AmCham South China · On the Sidelines of APEC 2026

AmCham South China Business Summit
& Philadelphia Orchestra Chamber Ensemble

On the sidelines of APEC 2026 — an afternoon of keynotes, three roundtables and a chamber concert.

📅 Tuesday, November 17, 2026 · 13:30–18:15 📍 Yongxinhui Xiagong, Nanshan, Shenzhen 🗣 All-English agenda

The Day

An all-English agenda with around 200 attendees. The heads of the three American chambers in China opened together; three roundtables covered the digital future, supply-chain security and regional cooperation; and the Philadelphia Orchestra chamber ensemble closed the day.

14:00 · Opening Remarks

The heads of all three U.S. chambers, on one stage

Dr. Harley Seyedin (AmCham South China), Eric Zheng (AmCham Shanghai) and James M. Zimmerman (AmCham China, Beijing) spoke in turn — the top leaders of the American chambers in South China, Shanghai and Beijing sharing one stage. That was the calibre of the room.

From 14:15 · Keynotes & Company Sessions

Energy, AI and Asia-Pacific markets

Rodrigo Diaz of ExxonMobil presented the 2026 Global Energy Outlook: by 2050 world population grows by about 1.5 billion (all of it in non-OECD countries) and GDP by about 80%, while energy-related CO₂ emissions fall only about 20%. His conclusion is the “and” equation: increase supply & reduce emissions — both at once, not either/or.

Javier Torá, Chairman of the Spanish Chamber (founder of ES Grupo, who started out manufacturing leather goods in Guangzhou), spoke on manufacturing in the age of AI. Frederic Barbier of France's SGD Pharma laid out country cards for Asia-Pacific pharmaceutical markets: China is a “two-speed market” — affordable generics and innovative drugs side by side, with innovation rising while prices are pressed down by centralized procurement.

Ivan Lu, Vice President at Kraft Heinz Asia: the Asian business spans more than 10 countries, with about 90% of products made locally — “From Farm to Table”: end-to-end AI deployment, trusted data sharing, quality and traceability.

15:20 · Roundtable I

The Digital Future: How AI Is Changing Trade

Moderated by Joe Chow of Kroll. Tim Wen (CEO of Allway Group, Treasurer of the Chamber) put it most plainly: international trade has shifted from “selling products” to “defining and developing markets”. AI can deliver market insight, customer discovery and global connection — but it cannot replace innovation, market judgment, or trust between people. Melinda Xu (Managing Director at Ankura) addressed U.S.–China trade from the policy and regulatory side; Charles Freeman, Senior Vice President for Asia at the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, was the policy weathervane in from Washington.

15:50 · Roundtable II

Secure Supply Chains, Stronger Economies

Moderated by Eddie Leung, founder of the GBA CEO CLUB, with its President Kenneth Wong and Vice President Philip Wong (Vice President for Asia Value Chain at Movado) at the table. EY Partner Scott Chen presented EY's SCRM supply-chain resilience scorecard: scores of 1–10 across five dimensions (components 25% / suppliers 20% / manufacturing 25% / network 20% / the remaining 10%), down to details like “single source + single factory”, supplier time-to-recover (TTR), and whether a business continuity plan is in place. A second slide showed a leading manufacturer's vulnerability matrix: the more exclusive, irreplaceable and opaque a material is, the higher the risk.

16:28 · Roundtable III

China's Role in Regional Cooperation

Four heads of foreign chambers in South China took the stage: Fabian Pacory (French Chamber), Fabian Blake (Vice President, European Union Chamber of Commerce in China), Greg Smith (Australian Chamber) and Alexander von Kaldenberg (Canadian Chamber).

17:00–18:15 · Closing & Concert

Philadelphia Orchestra Chamber Ensemble

After Seyedin's closing remarks, the Philadelphia Orchestra's chamber ensemble performed — part of the orchestra's Asia tour residency in China: a string ensemble, heard up close to end the day.

Key Takeaways

  • The “and” equation (ExxonMobil): grow supply and cut emissions at the same time — oil and gas will still account for about half of global energy in 2050.
  • The definition of trade has changed (Tim Wen): from selling products to defining markets; AI handles efficiency, trust closes the deal.
  • Single sourcing is the original sin (EY SCRM): big manufacturers are using scorecards to push supply chains toward dual plants, dual sources and certified backup factories.
  • 90% made locally (Kraft Heinz): the answer for multinational consumer brands in Asia is local manufacturing plus a digitally agile supply chain.
  • The scope of digital assets (Kroll): domains, accounts, IP and content all count as assets — a point that maps neatly onto anyone holding a portfolio of domains and accounts.

What the Summit Was About

1. The Greater Bay Area is APEC's gateway. The day's main thread: Shenzhen and the Greater Bay Area uniquely combine manufacturing, supply chains and technology in one place — the most practical ground for AI in this era. The edge is not frontier technology itself, but putting AI to work in real-world scenarios.

2. U.S.–China friction is structural, not cyclical. Tariffs sit at historic highs, and the definition of “national security” keeps expanding to AI, models and chips. U.S. firms are “rebalancing” their China presence — but speakers warned that those who pull out will hand their market share to the competitors who stay.

3. Energy must serve growth and emissions cuts at the same time. ExxonMobil's outlook: by 2050 world population grows by more than 1.5 billion and electricity demand by about 65%, while oil and gas still supply more than half of global energy; natural gas plays a bridging role in China. There is no single fix — the “energy equation” has to be solved with a diversified mix.

4. Supply chains are regionalizing; resilience matters more than cost. Intra-regional trade is now growing faster than global trade. The resilience framework looks at five things — resources and local content, supplier financial health, regional footprint, logistics-network correlation, and capacity and quality credentials. The real question is not how many backups you have, but how many truly independent, qualified alternatives there are; supply-chain finance should help SME suppliers stay alive.

5. AI's bottleneck is trust and compliance, not technology. Fragmented compliance regimes across countries force companies into over-compliance and siloed data architectures. The market is splitting — the gap between people who use AI well in real time and those who don't keeps widening. The suggestion: start with small-scale data-trust pilots in the Greater Bay Area.

6. Asia-Pacific markets are diverging. China's exports remain strong while domestic demand is soft and inflation low; drug price cuts of more than 15% are putting real pressure on pharmaceutical companies. Japan grows slowly but is marginally improving; South Korea's import structure complements China's; Germany is a high-quality export market.

7. Consumer & food: localization + AI scheduling — but emotional connection can't be replaced. About 90% of products in Asia are made locally; AI is already used for demand forecasting, production scheduling and traceability. But as speakers stressed, AI can raise efficiency — it cannot replace the emotional connection food carries. It takes “science + art” to grow the industry.
