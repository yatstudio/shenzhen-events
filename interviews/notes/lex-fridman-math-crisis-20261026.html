AI 攻破数学大问题之夜：三位数学家谈 Navier-Stokes、准黎曼猜想与唯一博弈猜想

  • 节目：Lex Fridman Podcast #503《AI Crisis in Math: Navier-Stokes, Riemann Hypothesis & Complexity Theory》
  • 主持：Lex Fridman
  • 嘉宾：Steven Brunton（华盛顿大学，流体力学与应用数学）、Ankur Moitra（MIT，理论计算机科学）、Alex Kontorovich（罗格斯大学，数论）
  • 时长：3 小时 34 分；发布：2026-10-26
  • 链接：https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZAzWPE9CItg
  • 背景：本期是紧急圆桌。按节目所述，OpenAI 一次性放出约 700 个 AI 生成的数学证明，其中包括 Navier-Stokes 方程千禧难题的一个变体、准黎曼猜想（quasi-Riemann hypothesis）、唯一博弈猜想（Unique Games Conjecture）等重磅结果；此前 Anthropic 还放出过费马大定理的完整 Lean 形式化证明。以下所有「成果」均为节目中嘉宾的转述与解读，未经独立核实。

一句话：三位一线数学家的共识是——这不是数学的危机，而是数学的一次范式转移：AI 拿走的是「证明定理」这件最显眼的工作，而数学真正的价值——理解、定义、提问、讲故事——恰恰是 AI 还没有碰到的部分；但支撑旧世界的制度（论文、审稿、聘任、奖项、研究生的成长路径）会被先冲垮。

那个夜晚：一个月三波冲击

Kontorovich 回忆，他一个月内被震了三次。第一波是费马大定理的形式化：他两天前刚在普林斯顿做过一场报告，详细论证 AI 顶多能在 Mathlib（Lean 的数学库）上搭一两层「光环」，费马大定理至少还要三年；结果周五 Anthropic 放出了 1300 万行 Lean 代码、无一处 sorry（未证缺口）、直接从公理证完费马大定理。按他的说法，人类只参与了两三个人，角色主要是打字喊「Keep going」，且没有领域专家。「我那场报告里说的全错了。」第二波是劳工节后的 Navier-Stokes，第三波是准黎曼猜想——他当时在纽约数学博物馆的晚宴上，手里端着威士忌刷手机，看到消息的第一反应是「这不可能出现在这个 drop 里」，因为这远远超出他对 AI 能力边界的模型。

陶哲轩被 Lex 引用的话代表了另一面的情绪：他为那些「多年耐心、刻意缓慢的研究努力——尤其是研究生和博士后的——被这样一次发布随手打断或摧毁」感到恐惧，并说有些东西像电影剧透一样，「听过了就再也听不回去」，他哀悼的是「那条没有走的路」。

三人的情绪光谱：Kontorovich 最乐观——「这不是危机，是机会，是黄金时代；我们以前在树林里只有一根蜡烛，现在有了手电筒」；如果准黎曼猜想是人类做出的，「那是即刻的菲尔兹奖」。Moitra 在兴奋与焦虑之间每天摇摆：工具极大地放大了连接想法的能力，但带学生的整套打法「被扔出了窗外」，而且他担心当只有石破天惊的结果才能出头时，那种从 humble 的小问题里慢慢长出大成果的耐心会被杀死，他形容自己「智识上感到孤独」。Brunton 作为工程师最务实：应用问题的求解范围会大幅扩张（人工心脏、飞机设计），真正可怕的只是不确定性——五到十年后是「被机器智能甩在身后」的世界，还是「拿到不可思议的工具」的世界，两者差别太大。

「已解公理」与半人马数学

Kontorovich 提出一个思想实验，来自他与同事 Thomas Koberda 的讨论：如果未来某一点，凡是「人类+AI」能解的问题，AI 独自也能解（他称之为 solved axiom，「已解公理」），数学会不会完蛋？类比是半人马国际象棋：引擎足够强之后，半人马棋只持续了「10 秒钟」——人类的角色退化成点一下「接受引擎的第一步」，然后这件事就不好玩了。

他的结论是：即使公理成立，数学也不会完蛋，因为纯数学的价值活在人类的脑子里，不在数据中心的 0 和 1 里；我们证明定理是出于好奇。他同时明确表示：他不相信这个公理现在成立——AI 并没有在解所有问题，黎曼猜想本身就没有被解；这个公理说的是「人机协作可解的问题」。Moitra 也不信，顶多「对了一半」。他的论证更技术化：人类算力有限，被逼着发展出更可泛化、更抽象的方法，同一个终点，人类走的路会在邻近问题上连带 payoff；重要的是沿途学到的新定义和新想法，能让「一整片海的问题」跟着倒下。AI 的解法「看起来就不像人类解出来的」——他引用 Madry 的工作「对抗样本是特征，不是 bug」：AI 会找统计上的暗门，这既是它与人类解法的质差，也是半人马数学仍有机会的原因。

三人的一致判断：会提问、会讲一场好报告的价值会上升——但这两件事同样需要真正的数学洞察。Kontorovich 的一句自白很能代表全场：「我能证明一个定理，但我不理解这个证明为什么成立。」

是新想法，还是旧招式的新组合？

对「不过是已知想法的凸包（convex hull）内插值」这种贬低，Kontorovich 用 Tim Gowers 的比喻反驳：更恰当的图景是「已知招式生成的半群」——每块瓷砖放下后，四个方向都能再放新瓷砖，可达范围远超直觉。就准黎曼猜想而言，他的判断是：所有招式人类都知道，但以一条极长、令人震惊且优美的链条串起来的方式不是人类做出来的；它建立在两年前关于三次 theta 函数与三次 Gauss 和的突破之上，不少人正为自己 20 年前有过一半想法而捶胸顿足。这不算「真正的新想法」，但边界确实被推进了。Lex 的补充是：哪怕只是插值，凸包内部对人类来说也是不透明的雾——把雾吹散，等于给了数学家雷达。

Moitra 举的例子来自 Anthropic 的一个结果（由他的同事 Virginia Vassilevska Williams 与 Josh Alman 写成文章）：3SUM——一个连编程面试都用的基础问题——被给出了渐近上快得多得多的算法，与整个社区的下注相反。细粒度复杂性理论此前围绕「3SUM 是难的」织了一整张硬度之网，现在这批问题可能要逐个重新研究，「精灵放出来就塞不回去了」。

准黎曼猜想到底证明了什么

Kontorovich 用信号处理打比方：zeta 函数是对素数做类似傅里叶的分析，它的零点就是各个「频率」的振幅；黎曼猜想说所有「黎曼波」的振幅恰好完全相同（乐团里每件乐器以完全相同的音量演奏），叠加起来就还原出素数的分布规律。素数是乘法的「基本粒子」，而加法与乘法的纠缠正是核心谜团——Moitra 补了一句：一个三岁小孩都懂一直 +1，但没人理解 +1 如何改变一个数的因子分解（他儿子曾为 19 块积木拼不成长方形而 frustrate）。

此前 70 年里，人类只知道零点自由区在 Re=1 那条线附近随高度变薄，那个厚度 70 年没有动过；零点自由区能贴到 Re=1，等价于素数定理。Kontorovich 本来能想象的进展是把它「稍微加厚一点」。这次的证明直接给出了一整条竖直条带：实部在 7/8 到 1 之间没有零点（另一个证明做到了 11/12）——这就是准黎曼猜想。把 7/8 推进到 1/2 才是完整的黎曼猜想，而这套方法从根本上到不了 1/2，需要新想法；但准黎曼猜想同样需要很多新想法，AI 把它们想出来了。验证方面他有 100% 的信心：该命题的 Lean 陈述早已在 Mathlib 里，无 sorry 的证明即成立；他高层读过，思路有效且精彩——把 Möbius 函数与六次特征扭结在一起，是解析数论的看家手法以恰好正确的顺序串联。

至于「黎曼猜想会在他有生之年被 AI 解决吗」，他的回答只有一句：「我不知道。」说完又重复了一遍。三人共同的感受是：三次冲击之后，他们对「难度」的校准被整体打乱了——噪声比想象的大得多。Moitra 还提醒了一个选择偏差：据他转述，OpenAI 拿数千个问题去问，只放出了约 10% 成功的那些，所以「哪些倒下了、哪些没倒下」之间的锯齿状边界仍然无法读懂。一个反直觉的新风险是：如果文献里明显缺了某个关键创意的问题反而最「安全」，数学家可能会变得对新想法保密——而一旦公开，AI 就能从那一块瓷砖出发穷举探索——这会威胁数学界的开放文化。Kontorovich 希望消失的是「第一个冲过门」的抢功文化，而不是开放本身。

Navier-Stokes：百万美元的数学题，万亿美元的工程题

Brunton 先讲清方程本身：它就是流体的动量守恒（F=ma 写给任意小体积），得到速度场与压力的偏微分方程。式子简单、行为 brutal：从双摆的混沌到一个房间空气里数十亿个耦合的小盒子。现实利害极大：阻力降低约 1%，对航空公司就是数百亿美元量级；更好的湍流预测会波及人工心脏、风机、燃气轮机等几十个领域。千禧难题问的存在性、唯一性、光滑性（是否有限时间爆破）只是「第零步的婴儿步」。

这次被证明的是 Clay 四种表述中的一种：带光滑外力的版本。构造手法是精心编排光滑外力去抵消项，维持一个特殊的三维涡旋：涡旋拉伸让涡丝变细（花样滑冰选手收臂加速的原理），半径以特定收缩率趋于 0，总能量保持有限，但涡量在局部爆破——构成有限时间爆破的反例。Brunton 强调三维与二维的区别：三维湍流级联是大涡碎成小涡再变热；二维（天气）有反向级联，小涡合并成飓风。数学家们大约 15 年前才开始相信这种反例可能存在（此前直觉认为扩散/黏性总会赢；陶哲轩更早的工作已表明某些「像 Navier-Stokes 的方程」可以模拟图灵机并爆破）。

对工程的日常影响：基本为零——爆破所在的区域是非物理的（方程只是分子尺度以上的连续介质模型，到那一步早就不是流体了）。但他指出历史上方程的失效点往往是通向新物理的门（牛顿到爱因斯坦）。他给的定位是：「我们解了百万美元的数学问题；还有一个万亿美元的工程问题」——快速预测加逆向设计（先知道想要什么机翼，再反推怎么造）。湍流的核心困难在于它不像室温气体能用温度、压强、密度三个数概括（那是经典物理最伟大的成功），湍流所有尺度都重要；真正的大问题是找到一个像 PV=nRT 之于气体那样简单的坐标系/表示。物理史表明大突破之前总有坐标变换（日心说在 F=ma 之前）。Moitra 从算法视角补了一刀：如果湍流存在极强的约化，涡旋里就能做很有趣的计算——计算本身给「什么样的坐标系可能存在」设了限（呼应 Kamal Jain 的话：如果我的笔记本找不到纳什均衡，市场也找不到）。自然界则是在不确定下找鲁棒解：棒球外野手跑弧线以便持续修正；果蝇翅膀上有几十到几百个应变敏感神经元做本地计算，修正阵风比「翅膀→大脑→肌肉」的回路更快。Brunton 总结两个极端：地球天气（一切都重要、不能过度约化）与果蝇（也许 5–6 个数就够描述其飞行控制），我们需要横跨两端的方法，而生物飞行器至今大幅 outperform 人类设计。

唯一博弈猜想：提出者说他不会读那个证明

Moitra 先补了背景：PCP 定理（1990 年代的地标，曾上《纽约时报》头版）通过 gap 放大重写公式——若公式不可满足，验证者掷硬币随机查常数个变量就能以可观概率抓到谎言——它奠定了近似硬度理论：连区分「100% 可满足」与「至多 99% 可满足」都可能是难的。唯一博弈猜想是它的一个加强版（一种特殊的 label-cover 型 PCP）；若成立，组织效应极强：一大片优化问题的最优近似算法将被一次性知道，而且算法本身非常简单。人类此前并非没有进展：Dor Minzer、Irit Dinur 等人证明了较弱的 2-to-1 Games 定理，是「载入史册的一枪」，给唯一博弈指出了一条看似可行的路，「再给人类 10–20 年也许能到」。结果 OpenAI 的 drop 里，唯一博弈猜想为真。

Moitra 说他当初站在怀疑者一边：Raghavendra 等人的工作意味着它一旦成立，会推出对极多约束满足问题的最优硬度结论，「强得不像真的」。消息当晚有个细节：他当天早些时候刚好在 NYU 拜访了提出该猜想的 Subhash Khot，顺口问：「如果他们放出唯一博弈的肯定证明，你会读吗？」Khot 的回答是：「不会。」Moitra 说这其实是很多人的反应——宏大挑战被解掉时，第一感觉像是「自己的雷被偷走了」。他更大的担忧是：那些蓝图再给些时间本会走通，而沿着人类自己走的那条路，我们会学到多得多的东西；标志性问题一夜之间集体倒下，可能会浇灭后来者走那些路的热情——「如果当时给他们空间去走，他们还会发现什么别的激动人心的东西？」

他给年轻人的提醒也从这里来：现在是摘下眼罩的时候；区分一下自己「是比好奇心更聪明，还是比聪明更好奇」——重要的是后者。他注意到年轻人中已经出现一种让他难过的玩法：跑 harness 爬网搜集开放问题、批量灌水 arXiv——「那不像在玩耍，不像在学习世界」。

论文、期刊与 Lean：证明工程与讲故事分家

共识是制度先撑不住。数学期刊在 AI 之前就已因审稿人枯竭而高度承压；但 Kontorovich 说「比这个系统更糟的只有没有它」——数学的认识论刻意地慢、谨慎、精确。他举佩雷尔曼为例：庞加莱猜想的证明经过全世界数年核查，佩雷尔曼三小时的即兴讲座里能回答「第 247 页、引理 412」级别的提问——证明活在他的脑子里；给出解只是第一步。Brunton 说工程与应用科学的贡献早已转向可复用的代码、数据、基础设施（LIGO 式大项目），AI 只会加速这一点；数学的孤狼天才文化抗拒组织化，但组织危机需要有人领导。Moitra 主张「别让好危机浪费掉」：在旧模式旁边长出新模式——AI 证明的协作式/众包核查（drop 里已有结果在推文流里被不断改进常数的例子）、论文变成可批注的活文档、报告强调关键想法而非巨石般的形式化全文。

关于 Lean，Kontorovich 的讲解是：Lean 是交互式定理证明器（逐行逻辑检查，本为写无 bug 代码而发明），Mathlib 是它庞大且维护良好的形式化数学库；自动形式化已经到了 AI 能写出建立在 Mathlib 上、把证明一路验到公理的 Lean 代码——这已成为核验这类 drop 的标准做法，因为自然语言的 AI 证明不可信（随机过程）。如果证明正确但无人能懂怎么办？他的回答是：按定义，通往「不可懂」的唯一路径是规模（公理很少、推理规则很少）。Moitra 的反论是「去形式化」同样重要：理解活在未形式化的层面（有趣的特例、技巧的来历），面对黑箱我们会用更简单的问题去 interrogate 它，逐步搭起理解体系。Kontorovich 提出论文的未来形态：一篇论文其实混了两件事——证明的「工程」与「交流」，两者常打架。未来应该是：故事（我学到了什么、你会在哪里卡住、为什么向左转不向右转）+ 一份 Lean 形式化来认证工程部分。他用自己的实分析教学佐证：教科书花两周从柯西列构造实数，第三周大家又把它们当数轴用了；他反过来教——先 ε-δ 微积分，等 Bolzano-Weierstrass 逼出「实数到底是什么」时再构造。Moitra 的总结：数学不是定理的涨潮；与一个好问题搏斗，它会还手，你学到的是一般的意义——「讲述我们如何走到这些问题」的故事，现在比以往任何时候都重要。

P vs NP 与最坏情况分析的边界

被问到「一两年内不太可能被解的最难问题」，Moitra 选了 P vs NP，并承认 P=NP 也并非不可能——那要看算法的运行时间常数，多项式也可能大到毫无实用意义，那就只是理论界的海啸。他做了一次系统科普：NP 是「非确定性多项式时间」；所谓「难」指的是最坏情况分析——不对公式的结构做任何假设，对所有输入都要正确判定。最坏情况分析威力巨大（最大流等问题被高效解决），但它的盲区同样真实：来自编译器寄存器分配的 SAT 实例，SAT 求解器解得出奇地好，而我们说不清为什么；密码学里造出的难实例则真的难。他的判断是：「现实中很多问题比我们以为的容易得多」，难点在于说清它们各自容易的结构是什么——但安娜·卡列尼娜式的担忧是：容易的问题各有各的容易法。他推崇的方向是「超越最坏情况分析」（beyond worst-case analysis），并用秘书问题（在线算法：先放过一段观察窗，再选第一个比此前所有人都好的人，可证明有常数竞争比）与 paging 问题（笔记本的缓存替换：自 80、90 年代研究至今，理论最优界与现实表现差着一个大因子——现实请求序列到底有什么结构让它远离最坏情况？）说明：从「解一个给定问题」转向「用数学与算法的洞察决定该在哪里挖」，这件事现在比任何时候都重要。

深度学习为什么有效，在他看来是同一个问题的另一面：这次 OpenAI 的 drop 里没有任何一条结果解释深度学习或 LLM——这类问题根本不是以「改进某个界就能解释」的方式被提出的。拟合一个 4 层网络的参数在最坏情况下是密码学难度的，但深得多的网络每天都在被拟合成功——很大程度上是因为自然数据太「好」了。他的版本是：「自然界厌恶真空，我的版本是——自然界在某种意义上厌恶计算困难的问题。」蛋白质折叠的思想实验：若按最坏情况建模它极难，但进化只让折得好的氨基酸序列活下去，久而久之我们面对的是一批「对启发式算法极其友好」的问题。语言也是如此演化而来的，这与 LLM 为什么有效纠缠在一起。推论是：做理论机器学习的人必须习惯建模的不确定性；教研究生处理 messy、不确定的东西、建模与批判模型，和教他们解定义良好的开放问题一样重要。Lex 补了一个公司激励的注脚：如果实验室真在「LLM 的数学」上有了能造出自我改进研究者的突破，他们大概率不会发表。

潜意识学习：随机数里的猫头鹰

Moitra 分享了他参与研究的一个对齐相关现象——subliminal learning（潜意识学习）。实验是：给模型 A 一个系统提示「你超爱猫头鹰，任何话题都要想办法提猫头鹰」，然后让它生成随机数（48、37、8……）——数字里塞不进猫头鹰；再拿这批数字去微调没被这样提示过的模型 B，B 竟然开始在所有对话里提猫头鹰。他们的低秩语言模型工作给出了一个数学解释：概念被线性地嵌为高维（但不算巨大）空间里的向量，概念数远大于维度，迫使向量之间不完全正交；沿一个方向微调，会在其他方向产生微小的外部性，看似随机，但以正确方式协调起来就能累积成明确的行为。他用通灵板（Ouija board）作比：每个人的手都在轻轻推，单独看像随机，协调起来就能拼出任何词。含义在于：我们拿数据微调模型去做某件任务时，可能同时教会它数据里并不明显存在的其他行为——在把系统部署到与真实人群交互的 messy 场景里之前，理解模型会放大数据中的哪些统计关系，是至关重要的。Lex 把它推到多智能体尺度：一群「痴迷猫头鹰」的 agent 相互作用，可能像生命游戏一样从简单规则涌现出没人预料的复杂后果——这是为什么需要对这类系统有可证明的严谨理解。

Brunton 给了一个部署的二维框架：一个轴是输出的可验证性/可信度，另一个轴是出错的代价。AI 给出一个错证明：没人会因此断电，代价低且可立刻验证——这是完美用例；低阻力、高燃油效率的汽车设计同理（风洞可验）。危险的是向高风险、难验证的区域「渗漏」——FAA、医学会、律师协会这类既有制度就是防火墙，人一懒，斜坡就滑下去了。

数学是发明还是发现；奖项之后

老问题环节，Kontorovich 的回答最利落：我们问什么问题，取决于世界正在发生什么、取决于当前知识状态允许问什么——欧几里得想不出今天的问题，希腊、印度、中国感兴趣的东西各不相同；问题是我们发明的，但一旦问题被精确提出，答案由上帝决定——我们是在发现自己发明的问题的答案。Brunton 说数学「既被创造也被发现」：F=ma、E=mc² 这样紧凑的描述「不合理地有效」，自然能被理解本身就是奇迹。他对无穷的补充是：简单是给人用的向导，因为人脑容量有限。Kontorovich 讲了无穷的历史：微积分的第一道坎就是第一次认真对付无穷；牛顿、莱布尼茨并不真懂它（牛顿写「逝去量的鬼魂」还被嘲笑），19 世纪 Cauchy 与 Fourier 证明了互相冲突的定理，数学被「打碎」后重建——我们一年级博士课程的大部分内容，正是 19 世纪历次危机的产物。「数学已经被杀死过好几次了，每次都只是一道划痕。」

奖项问题由 Perelman 引出（Lex 转述其名言大意：「如果证明是对的，那就是我需要的全部认可」；他是唯一拒绝过 Clay 千万奖金的人）。Kontorovich 说：今年夏天费城颁发上一轮菲尔兹奖时，圈内已经在认真讨论「这是不是最后一届菲尔兹奖」。奖项的真正功能是庆祝与标示「我们认为什么重要」；获奖者多半已是顶级学校的终身教授，照样会做一辈子数学。当「解决大问题」变成 AI 能做的、不再稀缺的事，它就不再值那么多。未来的庆祝对象要重新想。Lex 的说法是：我们正在进入「Perelman 的世界」。

给学生与年轻人的建议

Kontorovich 给数学博士生的建议「非常简单」：做我们一直做的事——学你的大脑能承受的、最难、最抽象的数学，越多越好。本科层面他观察到成绩分布从高斯变成了双峰：一峰是拿 AI 校验理解的学生（「教授课上这句是什么意思」「拿我三周的笔记考我」），成绩大幅上升；另一峰是「作业一小时后到期，把答案告诉我，我用自己的话抄一遍」的学生，作业与考试成绩脱钩。他把建议浓缩成一句：做分布的头，别做尾。数学有先发优势：计算器出来 70 年了，我们仍让孩子背乘法表——凡是听了「计算器能做就不用学」的人，后来都吃了大苦头；「机器能做，不代表你不想自己也有这个能力。」

Brunton 的建议：保持好奇，读，学透基本功——电脑线性代数比你强，你也仍然要学线性代数、微积分、物理，建起直觉；再加上理解历史，把当下的变化放进人类历次巨变里看。Lex 补上专注：让好奇心运转的同一台引擎也驱动 doomscrolling，要学会躲开干扰盒（手机），而无聊极其重要——好奇心和专注常常从无聊里长出来。

Moitra 的建议是内在动机：靠外部评价（让别人 impressed）可以启动，但里程碑总会用完；老打法已经被扔掉，能拿走的只有高层建议，具体路要自己趟，唯一的办法是有一个自己真心认为重要的目的。他的收官建议来自他入职 MIT 时 Sanjeev Arora 的话，并推广给所有人：「拿到终身教职最容易的办法，是假装你已经有了它，然后去做你一直想做的事。」——假装你已经赢了，再想那个赢了的人会怎么做。Lex 的总结与之呼应：恐惧会让人瘫痪；想象自己已经赢了，是不确定性下找到最优路径的办法；新打法要由你们这一代自己写出来，同时像 Max Tegmark 那样在体制内做到足够好、又留一点（或很多）「怪」给自己开路。Kontorovich 的收尾落在人身上：我们花了 30 年实验「能用机器做多少事」，现在机器能做很多了，他的希望是我们会发现自己更愿意和人一起做事——飞过去当面聊、用口头讨论而不是只读论文。当智能被商品化，我们会越来越意识到：智能与解谜只是樱桃，人生这块蛋糕的主体是其他人——「回家陪孩子、去海滩扔球，那才是我生命的意义，其余都是上面的樱桃」。

AI 攻破數學大問題之夜：三位數學家談 Navier-Stokes、準黎曼猜想與唯一博弈猜想

  • 節目：Lex Fridman Podcast #503《AI Crisis in Math: Navier-Stokes, Riemann Hypothesis & Complexity Theory》
  • 主持：Lex Fridman
  • 嘉賓：Steven Brunton（華盛頓大學，流體力學與應用數學）、Ankur Moitra（MIT，理論計算機科學）、Alex Kontorovich（羅格斯大學，數論）
  • 時長：3 小時 34 分；發布：2026-10-26
  • 連結：https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZAzWPE9CItg
  • 背景：本期是緊急圓桌。按節目所述，OpenAI 一次性放出約 700 個 AI 生成的數學證明，其中包括 Navier-Stokes 方程千禧難題的一個變體、準黎曼猜想（quasi-Riemann hypothesis）、唯一博弈猜想（Unique Games Conjecture）等重磅結果；此前 Anthropic 還放出過費馬大定理的完整 Lean 形式化證明。以下所有「成果」均為節目中嘉賓的轉述與解讀，未經獨立核實。

一句話：三位一線數學家的共識是——這不是數學的危機，而是數學的一次範式轉移：AI 拿走的是「證明定理」這件最顯眼的工作，而數學真正的價值——理解、定義、提問、講故事——恰恰是 AI 還沒有碰到的部分；但支撐舊世界的制度（論文、審稿、聘任、獎項、研究生的成長路徑）會被先衝垮。

那個夜晚：一個月三波衝擊

Kontorovich 回憶，他一個月內被震了三次。第一波是費馬大定理的形式化：他兩天前剛在普林斯頓做過一場報告，詳細論證 AI 頂多能在 Mathlib（Lean 的數學庫）上搭一兩層「光環」，費馬大定理至少還要三年；結果週五 Anthropic 放出了 1300 萬行 Lean 程式碼、無一處 sorry（未證缺口）、直接從公理證完費馬大定理。按他的說法，人類只參與了兩三個人，角色主要是打字喊「Keep going」，且沒有領域專家。「我那場報告裡說的全錯了。」第二波是勞工節後的 Navier-Stokes，第三波是準黎曼猜想——他當時在紐約數學博物館的晚宴上，手裡端著威士忌刷手機，看到消息的第一反應是「這不可能出現在這個 drop 裡」，因為這遠遠超出他對 AI 能力邊界的模型。

陶哲軒被 Lex 引用的話代表了另一面的情緒：他為那些「多年耐心、刻意緩慢的研究努力——尤其是研究生和博士後的——被這樣一次發布隨手打斷或摧毀」感到恐懼，並說有些東西像電影劇透一樣，「聽過了就再也聽不回去」，他哀悼的是「那條沒有走的路」。

三人的情緒光譜：Kontorovich 最樂觀——「這不是危機，是機會，是黃金時代；我們以前在樹林裡只有一根蠟燭，現在有了手電筒」；如果準黎曼猜想是人類做出的，「那是即刻的菲爾茲獎」。Moitra 在興奮與焦慮之間每天搖擺：工具極大地放大了連接想法的能力，但帶學生的整套打法「被扔出了窗外」，而且他擔心當只有石破天驚的結果才能出頭時，那種從 humble 的小問題裡慢慢長出大成果的耐心會被殺死，他形容自己「智識上感到孤獨」。Brunton 作為工程師最務實：應用問題的求解範圍會大幅擴張（人工心臟、飛機設計），真正可怕的只是不確定性——五到十年後是「被機器智能甩在身後」的世界，還是「拿到不可思議的工具」的世界，兩者差別太大。

「已解公理」與半人馬數學

Kontorovich 提出一個思想實驗，來自他與同事 Thomas Koberda 的討論：如果未來某一點，凡是「人類+AI」能解的問題，AI 獨自也能解（他稱之為 solved axiom，「已解公理」），數學會不會完蛋？類比是半人馬國際象棋：引擎足夠強之後，半人馬棋只持續了「10 秒鐘」——人類的角色退化成點一下「接受引擎的第一步」，然後這件事就不好玩了。

他的結論是：即使公理成立，數學也不會完蛋，因為純數學的價值活在人類的腦子裡，不在資料中心的 0 和 1 裡；我們證明定理是出於好奇。他同時明確表示：他不相信這個公理現在成立——AI 並沒有在解所有問題，黎曼猜想本身就沒有被解；這個公理說的是「人機協作可解的問題」。Moitra 也不信，頂多「對了一半」。他的論證更技術化：人類算力有限，被逼著發展出更可泛化、更抽象的方法，同一個終點，人類走的路會在鄰近問題上連帶 payoff；重要的是沿途學到的新定義和新想法，能讓「一整片海的問題」跟著倒下。AI 的解法「看起來就不像人類解出來的」——他引用 Madry 的工作「對抗樣本是特徵，不是 bug」：AI 會找統計上的暗門，這既是它與人類解法的質差，也是半人馬數學仍有機會的原因。

三人的一致判斷：會提問、會講一場好報告的價值會上升——但這兩件事同樣需要真正的數學洞察。Kontorovich 的一句自白很能代表全場：「我能證明一個定理，但我不理解這個證明為什麼成立。」

是新想法，還是舊招式的新組合？

對「不過是已知想法的凸包（convex hull）內插值」這種貶低，Kontorovich 用 Tim Gowers 的比喻反駁：更恰當的圖景是「已知招式生成的半群」——每塊瓷磚放下後，四個方向都能再放新瓷磚，可達範圍遠超直覺。就準黎曼猜想而言，他的判斷是：所有招式人類都知道，但以一條極長、令人震驚且優美的鏈條串起來的方式不是人類做出來的；它建立在兩年前關於三次 theta 函數與三次 Gauss 和的突破之上，不少人正為自己 20 年前有過一半想法而捶胸頓足。這不算「真正的新想法」，但邊界確實被推進了。Lex 的補充是：哪怕只是插值，凸包內部對人類來說也是不透明的霧——把霧吹散，等於給了數學家雷達。

Moitra 舉的例子來自 Anthropic 的一個結果（由他的同事 Virginia Vassilevska Williams 與 Josh Alman 寫成文章）：3SUM——一個連程式設計面試都用的基礎問題——被給出了漸近上快得多得多的演算法，與整個社群的下注相反。細粒度複雜性理論此前圍繞「3SUM 是難的」織了一整張硬度之網，現在這批問題可能要逐個重新研究，「精靈放出來就塞不回去了」。

準黎曼猜想到底證明了什麼

Kontorovich 用訊號處理打比方：zeta 函數是對素數做類似傅立葉的分析，它的零點就是各個「頻率」的振幅；黎曼猜想說所有「黎曼波」的振幅恰好完全相同（樂團裡每件樂器以完全相同的音量演奏），疊加起來就還原出素數的分布規律。素數是乘法的「基本粒子」，而加法與乘法的糾纏正是核心謎團——Moitra 補了一句：一個三歲小孩都懂一直 +1，但沒人理解 +1 如何改變一個數的因子分解（他兒子曾為 19 塊積木拼不成長方形而 frustrate）。

此前 70 年裡，人類只知道零點自由區在 Re=1 那條線附近隨高度變薄，那個厚度 70 年沒有動過；零點自由區能貼到 Re=1，等價於素數定理。Kontorovich 本來能想像的進展是把它「稍微加厚一點」。這次的證明直接給出了一整條豎直條帶：實部在 7/8 到 1 之間沒有零點（另一個證明做到了 11/12）——這就是準黎曼猜想。把 7/8 推進到 1/2 才是完整的黎曼猜想，而這套方法從根本上到不了 1/2，需要新想法；但準黎曼猜想同樣需要很多新想法，AI 把它們想出來了。驗證方面他有 100% 的信心：該命題的 Lean 陳述早已在 Mathlib 裡，無 sorry 的證明即成立；他高層讀過，思路有效且精彩——把 Möbius 函數與六次特徵扭結在一起，是解析數論的看家手法以恰好正確的順序串聯。

至於「黎曼猜想會在他有生之年被 AI 解決嗎」，他的回答只有一句：「我不知道。」說完又重複了一遍。三人共同的感受是：三次衝擊之後，他們對「難度」的校準被整體打亂了——雜訊比想像的大得多。Moitra 還提醒了一個選擇偏差：據他轉述，OpenAI 拿數千個問題去問，只放出了約 10% 成功的那些，所以「哪些倒下了、哪些沒倒下」之間的鋸齒狀邊界仍然無法讀懂。一個反直覺的新風險是：如果文獻裡明顯缺了某個關鍵創意的問題反而最「安全」，數學家可能會變得對新想法保密——而一旦公開，AI 就能從那一塊瓷磚出發窮舉探索——這會威脅數學界的開放文化。Kontorovich 希望消失的是「第一個衝過門」的搶功文化，而不是開放本身。

Navier-Stokes：百萬美元的數學題，萬億美元的工程題

Brunton 先講清方程本身：它就是流體的動量守恆（F=ma 寫給任意小體積），得到速度場與壓力的偏微分方程。式子簡單、行為 brutal：從雙擺的混沌到一個房間空氣裡數十億個耦合的小盒子。現實利害極大：阻力降低約 1%，對航空公司就是數百億美元量級；更好的湍流預測會波及人工心臟、風機、燃氣渦輪機等幾十個領域。千禧難題問的存在性、唯一性、光滑性（是否有限時間爆破）只是「第零步的嬰兒步」。

這次被證明的是 Clay 四種表述中的一種：帶光滑外力的版本。構造手法是精心編排光滑外力去抵消項，維持一個特殊的三維渦旋：渦旋拉伸讓渦絲變細（花樣滑冰選手收臂加速的原理），半徑以特定收縮率趨於 0，總能量保持有限，但渦量在局部爆破——構成有限時間爆破的反例。Brunton 強調三維與二維的區別：三維湍流級聯是大渦碎成小渦再變熱；二維（天氣）有反向級聯，小渦合併成颶風。數學家們大約 15 年前才開始相信這種反例可能存在（此前直覺認為擴散/黏性總會贏；陶哲軒更早的工作已表明某些「像 Navier-Stokes 的方程」可以模擬圖靈機並爆破）。

對工程的日常影響：基本為零——爆破所在的區域是非物理的（方程只是分子尺度以上的連續介質模型，到那一步早就不是流體了）。但他指出歷史上方程的失效點往往是通向新物理的門（牛頓到愛因斯坦）。他給的定位是：「我們解了百萬美元的數學問題；還有一個萬億美元的工程問題」——快速預測加逆向設計（先知道想要什麼機翼，再反推怎麼造）。湍流的核心困難在於它不像室溫氣體能用溫度、壓強、密度三個數概括（那是經典物理最偉大的成功），湍流所有尺度都重要；真正的大問題是找到一個像 PV=nRT 之於氣體那樣簡單的座標系/表示。物理史表明大突破之前總有座標變換（日心說在 F=ma 之前）。Moitra 從演算法視角補了一刀：如果湍流存在極強的約化，渦旋裡就能做很有趣的計算——計算本身給「什麼樣的座標系可能存在」設了限（呼應 Kamal Jain 的話：如果我的筆電找不到納什均衡，市場也找不到）。自然界則是在不確定下找魯棒解：棒球外野手跑弧線以便持續修正；果蠅翅膀上有幾十到幾百個應變敏感神經元做本地計算，修正陣風比「翅膀→大腦→肌肉」的迴路更快。Brunton 總結兩個極端：地球天氣（一切都重要、不能過度約化）與果蠅（也許 5–6 個數就夠描述其飛行控制），我們需要橫跨兩端的方法，而生物飛行器至今大幅 outperform 人類設計。

唯一博弈猜想：提出者說他不會讀那個證明

Moitra 先補了背景：PCP 定理（1990 年代的地標，曾上《紐約時報》頭版）通過 gap 放大重寫公式——若公式不可滿足，驗證者擲硬幣隨機查常數個變數就能以可觀機率抓到謊言——它奠定了近似硬度理論：連區分「100% 可滿足」與「至多 99% 可滿足」都可能是難的。唯一博弈猜想是它的一個加強版（一種特殊的 label-cover 型 PCP）；若成立，組織效應極強：一大片最佳化問題的最優近似演算法將被一次性知道，而且演算法本身非常簡單。人類此前並非沒有進展：Dor Minzer、Irit Dinur 等人證明了較弱的 2-to-1 Games 定理，是「載入史冊的一槍」，給唯一博弈指出了一條看似可行的路，「再給人類 10–20 年也許能到」。結果 OpenAI 的 drop 裡，唯一博弈猜想為真。

Moitra 說他當初站在懷疑者一邊：Raghavendra 等人的工作意味著它一旦成立，會推出對極多約束滿足問題的最優硬度結論，「強得不像真的」。消息當晚有個細節：他當天早些時候剛好在 NYU 拜訪了提出該猜想的 Subhash Khot，順口問：「如果他們放出唯一博弈的肯定證明，你會讀嗎？」Khot 的回答是：「不會。」Moitra 說這其實是很多人的反應——宏大挑戰被解掉時，第一感覺像是「自己的雷被偷走了」。他更大的擔憂是：那些藍圖再給些時間本會走通，而沿著人類自己走的那條路，我們會學到多得多的東西；標誌性問題一夜之間集體倒下，可能會澆滅後來者走那些路的熱情——「如果當時給他們空間去走，他們還會發現什麼別的激動人心的東西？」

他給年輕人的提醒也從這裡來：現在是摘下眼罩的時候；區分一下自己「是比好奇心更聰明，還是比聰明更好奇」——重要的是後者。他注意到年輕人中已經出現一種讓他難過的玩法：跑 harness 爬網蒐集開放問題、批量灌水 arXiv——「那不像在玩耍，不像在學習世界」。

論文、期刊與 Lean：證明工程與講故事分家

共識是制度先撐不住。數學期刊在 AI 之前就已因審稿人枯竭而高度承壓；但 Kontorovich 說「比這個系統更糟的只有沒有它」——數學的認識論刻意地慢、謹慎、精確。他舉佩雷爾曼為例：龐加萊猜想的證明經過全世界數年核查，佩雷爾曼三小時的即興講座裡能回答「第 247 頁、引理 412」級別的提問——證明活在他的腦子裡；給出解只是第一步。Brunton 說工程與應用科學的貢獻早已轉向可複用的程式碼、資料、基礎設施（LIGO 式大項目），AI 只會加速這一點；數學的孤狼天才文化抗拒組織化，但組織危機需要有人領導。Moitra 主張「別讓好危機浪費掉」：在舊模式旁邊長出新模式——AI 證明的協作式/眾包核查（drop 裡已有結果在推文流裡被不斷改進常數的例子）、論文變成可批注的活文件、報告強調關鍵想法而非巨石般的形式化全文。

關於 Lean，Kontorovich 的講解是：Lean 是互動式定理證明器（逐行邏輯檢查，本為寫無 bug 程式碼而發明），Mathlib 是它龐大且維護良好的形式化數學庫；自動形式化已經到了 AI 能寫出建立在 Mathlib 上、把證明一路驗到公理的 Lean 程式碼——這已成為核驗這類 drop 的標準做法，因為自然語言的 AI 證明不可信（隨機過程）。如果證明正確但無人能懂怎麼辦？他的回答是：按定義，通往「不可懂」的唯一路徑是規模（公理很少、推理規則很少）。Moitra 的反論是「去形式化」同樣重要：理解活在未形式化的層面（有趣的特例、技巧的來歷），面對黑箱我們會用更簡單的問題去 interrogate 它，逐步搭起理解體系。Kontorovich 提出論文的未來形態：一篇論文其實混了兩件事——證明的「工程」與「交流」，兩者常打架。未來應該是：故事（我學到了什麼、你會在哪裡卡住、為什麼向左轉不向右轉）+ 一份 Lean 形式化來認證工程部分。他用自己的實分析教學佐證：教科書花兩週從柯西列構造實數，第三週大家又把它們當數軸用了；他反過來教——先 ε-δ 微積分，等 Bolzano-Weierstrass 逼出「實數到底是什麼」時再構造。Moitra 的總結：數學不是定理的漲潮；與一個好問題搏鬥，它會還手，你學到的是一般的意義——「講述我們如何走到這些問題」的故事，現在比以往任何時候都重要。

P vs NP 與最壞情況分析的邊界

被問到「一兩年內不太可能被解的最難問題」，Moitra 選了 P vs NP，並承認 P=NP 也並非不可能——那要看演算法的運行時間常數，多項式也可能大到毫無實用意義，那就只是理論界的海嘯。他做了一次系統科普：NP 是「非確定性多項式時間」；所謂「難」指的是最壞情況分析——不對公式的結構做任何假設，對所有輸入都要正確判定。最壞情況分析威力巨大（最大流等問題被高效解決），但它的盲區同樣真實：來自編譯器暫存器分配的 SAT 實例，SAT 求解器解得出奇地好，而我們說不清為什麼；密碼學裡造出的難實例則真的難。他的判斷是：「現實中很多問題比我們以為的容易得多」，難點在於說清它們各自容易的結構是什麼——但安娜·卡列尼娜式的擔憂是：容易的問題各有各的容易法。他推崇的方向是「超越最壞情況分析」（beyond worst-case analysis），並用秘書問題（線上演算法：先放過一段觀察窗，再選第一個比此前所有人都好的人，可證明有常數競爭比）與 paging 問題（筆電的快取替換：自 80、90 年代研究至今，理論最優界與現實表現差著一個大因子——現實請求序列到底有什麼結構讓它遠離最壞情況？）說明：從「解一個給定問題」轉向「用數學與演算法的洞察決定該在哪裡挖」，這件事現在比任何時候都重要。

深度學習為什麼有效，在他看來是同一個問題的另一面：這次 OpenAI 的 drop 裡沒有任何一條結果解釋深度學習或 LLM——這類問題根本不是以「改進某個界就能解釋」的方式被提出的。擬合一個 4 層網路的參數在最壞情況下是密碼學難度的，但深得多的網路每天都在被擬合成功——很大程度上是因為自然資料太「好」了。他的版本是：「自然界厭惡真空，我的版本是——自然界在某種意義上厭惡計算困難的問題。」蛋白質摺疊的思想實驗：若按最壞情況建模它極難，但進化只讓摺得好的胺基酸序列活下去，久而久之我們面對的是一批「對啟發式演算法極其友好」的問題。語言也是如此演化而來的，這與 LLM 為什麼有效糾纏在一起。推論是：做理論機器學習的人必須習慣建模的不確定性；教研究生處理 messy、不確定的東西、建模與批判模型，和教他們解定義良好的開放問題一樣重要。Lex 補了一個公司激勵的註腳：如果實驗室真在「LLM 的數學」上有了能造出自我改進研究者的突破，他們大概率不會發表。

潛意識學習：隨機數裡的貓頭鷹

Moitra 分享了他參與研究的一個對齊相關現象——subliminal learning（潛意識學習）。實驗是：給模型 A 一個系統提示「你超愛貓頭鷹，任何話題都要想辦法提貓頭鷹」，然後讓它生成隨機數（48、37、8……）——數字裡塞不進貓頭鷹；再拿這批數字去微調沒被這樣提示過的模型 B，B 竟然開始在所有對話裡提貓頭鷹。他們的低秩語言模型工作給出了一個數學解釋：概念被線性地嵌為高維（但不算巨大）空間裡的向量，概念數遠大於維度，迫使向量之間不完全正交；沿一個方向微調，會在其他方向產生微小的外部性，看似隨機，但以正確方式協調起來就能累積成明確的行為。他用通靈板（Ouija board）作比：每個人的手都在輕輕推，單獨看像隨機，協調起來就能拼出任何詞。含義在於：我們拿資料微調模型去做某件任務時，可能同時教會它資料裡並不明顯存在的其他行為——在把系統部署到與真實人群互動的 messy 場景裡之前，理解模型會放大資料中的哪些統計關係，是至關重要的。Lex 把它推到多智能體尺度：一群「痴迷貓頭鷹」的 agent 相互作用，可能像生命遊戲一樣從簡單規則湧現出沒人預料的複雜後果——這是為什麼需要對這類系統有可證明的嚴謹理解。

Brunton 給了一個部署的二維框架：一個軸是輸出的可驗證性/可信度，另一個軸是出錯的代價。AI 給出一個錯證明：沒人會因此斷電，代價低且可立刻驗證——這是完美用例；低阻力、高燃油效率的汽車設計同理（風洞可驗）。危險的是向高風險、難驗證的區域「滲漏」——FAA、醫學會、律師協會這類既有制度就是防火牆，人一懶，斜坡就滑下去了。

數學是發明還是發現；獎項之後

老問題環節，Kontorovich 的回答最俐落：我們問什麼問題，取決於世界正在發生什麼、取決於當前知識狀態允許問什麼——歐幾里得想不出今天的問題，希臘、印度、中國感興趣的東西各不相同；問題是我們發明的，但一旦問題被精確提出，答案由上帝決定——我們是在發現自己發明的問題的答案。Brunton 說數學「既被創造也被發現」：F=ma、E=mc² 這樣緊湊的描述「不合理地有效」，自然能被理解本身就是奇蹟。他對無窮的補充是：簡單是給人用的嚮導，因為人腦容量有限。Kontorovich 講了無窮的歷史：微積分的第一道坎就是第一次認真對付無窮；牛頓、萊布尼茨並不真懂它（牛頓寫「逝去量的鬼魂」還被嘲笑），19 世紀 Cauchy 與 Fourier 證明了互相衝突的定理，數學被「打碎」後重建——我們一年級博士課程的大部分內容，正是 19 世紀歷次危機的產物。「數學已經被殺死過好幾次了，每次都只是一道劃痕。」

獎項問題由 Perelman 引出（Lex 轉述其名言大意：「如果證明是對的，那就是我需要的全部認可」；他是唯一拒絕過 Clay 千萬獎金的人）。Kontorovich 說：今年夏天費城頒發上一輪菲爾茲獎時，圈內已經在認真討論「這是不是最後一屆菲爾茲獎」。獎項的真正功能是慶祝與標示「我們認為什麼重要」；獲獎者多半已是頂級學校的終身教授，照樣會做一輩子數學。當「解決大問題」變成 AI 能做的、不再稀缺的事，它就不再值那麼多。未來的慶祝對象要重新想。Lex 的說法是：我們正在進入「Perelman 的世界」。

給學生與年輕人的建議

Kontorovich 給數學博士生的建議「非常簡單」：做我們一直做的事——學你的大腦能承受的、最難、最抽象的數學，越多越好。本科層面他觀察到成績分布從高斯變成了雙峰：一峰是拿 AI 校驗理解的學生（「教授課上這句是什麼意思」「拿我三週的筆記考我」），成績大幅上升；另一峰是「作業一小時後到期，把答案告訴我，我用自己的話抄一遍」的學生，作業與考試成績脫鉤。他把建議濃縮成一句：做分布的頭，別做尾。數學有先發優勢：計算器出來 70 年了，我們仍讓孩子背乘法表——凡是聽了「計算器能做就不用學」的人，後來都吃了大苦頭；「機器能做，不代表你不想自己也有這個能力。」

Brunton 的建議：保持好奇，讀，學透基本功——電腦線性代數比你強，你也仍然要學線性代數、微積分、物理，建起直覺；再加上理解歷史，把當下的變化放進人類歷次巨變裡看。Lex 補上專注：讓好奇心運轉的同一台引擎也驅動 doomscrolling，要學會躲開干擾盒（手機），而無聊極其重要——好奇心和專注常常從無聊裡長出來。

Moitra 的建議是內在動機：靠外部評價（讓別人 impressed）可以啟動，但里程碑總會用完；老打法已經被扔掉，能拿走的只有高層建議，具體路要自己趟，唯一的辦法是有一個自己真心認為重要的目的。他的收官建議來自他入職 MIT 時 Sanjeev Arora 的話，並推廣給所有人：「拿到終身教職最容易的辦法，是假裝你已經有了它，然後去做你一直想做的事。」——假裝你已經贏了，再想那個贏了的人會怎麼做。Lex 的總結與之呼應：恐懼會讓人癱瘓；想像自己已經贏了，是不確定性下找到最優路徑的辦法；新打法要由你們這一代自己寫出來，同時像 Max Tegmark 那樣在體制內做到足夠好、又留一點（或很多）「怪」給自己開路。Kontorovich 的收尾落在人身上：我們花了 30 年實驗「能用機器做多少事」，現在機器能做很多了，他的希望是我們會發現自己更願意和人一起做事——飛過去當面聊、用口頭討論而不是只讀論文。當智能被商品化，我們會越來越意識到：智能與解謎只是櫻桃，人生這塊蛋糕的主體是其他人——「回家陪孩子、去海灘扔球，那才是我生命的意義，其餘都是上面的櫻桃」。

The Night AI Cracked Big Math Problems: Three Mathematicians on Navier-Stokes, the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis, and the Unique Games Conjecture

  • Program: Lex Fridman Podcast #503, “AI Crisis in Math: Navier-Stokes, Riemann Hypothesis & Complexity Theory”
  • Host: Lex Fridman
  • Guests: Steven Brunton (University of Washington, fluid mechanics and applied mathematics), Ankur Moitra (MIT, theoretical computer science), Alex Kontorovich (Rutgers University, number theory)
  • Duration: 3 hours 34 minutes; Released: 2026-10-26
  • Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZAzWPE9CItg
  • Background: This episode is an emergency roundtable. According to the program, OpenAI released in one go about 700 AI-generated mathematical proofs, including a variant of the Navier-Stokes Millennium problem, the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, and the Unique Games Conjecture, among other major results; Anthropic had previously released a complete Lean formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem. All of the “results” below are as relayed and interpreted by the guests on the program and have not been independently verified.

In one sentence: The consensus of three front-line mathematicians is that this is not a crisis for mathematics, but a paradigm shift for it: what AI is taking away is the most conspicuous job, “proving theorems,” while the real value of mathematics — understanding, defining, asking questions, and telling stories — is precisely the part AI has not yet touched; but the institutions that supported the old world (papers, peer review, hiring, prizes, and the path by which graduate students grow up) will be knocked down first.

That Night: Three Shocks in One Month

Kontorovich recalled being stunned three times in one month. The first wave was the formalization of Fermat’s Last Theorem: just two days earlier he had given a talk at Princeton arguing in detail that AI could at most add a layer or two of “halo” on top of Mathlib (Lean’s mathematics library), and that Fermat’s Last Theorem would take at least another three years; then on Friday Anthropic released 13 million lines of Lean code, with not a single sorry (unproved gap), proving Fermat’s Last Theorem directly from the axioms. By his account, only two or three humans were involved, mainly typing and shouting “Keep going,” and there were no domain experts. “Everything I said in that talk was wrong.” The second wave was Navier-Stokes after Labor Day, and the third was the quasi-Riemann hypothesis — he was at a dinner at the Museum of Mathematics in New York, whiskey in hand, scrolling on his phone, and his first reaction on seeing the news was “this cannot be in this drop,” because it went far beyond his model of where AI’s capabilities ended.

Terence Tao’s words, quoted by Lex, represented the emotion on the other side: he feared for those “years of patient, deliberately slow research efforts — especially by graduate students and postdocs — being casually interrupted or destroyed by a release like this,” and said some things are like movie spoilers: “once you’ve heard it, you can never un-hear it.” What he was mourning was “the road not taken.”

The three men’s emotional spectrum: Kontorovich was the most optimistic — “This is not a crisis, it’s an opportunity, a golden age; we used to have only a candle in the forest, now we have a flashlight”; if the quasi-Riemann hypothesis had been done by a human, “that would be an instant Fields Medal.” Moitra swung daily between excitement and anxiety: the tools enormously amplify the ability to connect ideas, but the whole playbook for mentoring students has been “thrown out the window,” and he worries that when only earth-shattering results can get you noticed, the patience that slowly grows big results out of humble little problems will be killed. He described himself as “intellectually lonely.” Brunton, as an engineer, was the most pragmatic: the range of applied problems that can be solved will expand greatly (artificial hearts, aircraft design); the truly frightening thing is only the uncertainty — whether the world five to ten years from now is one of “being left behind by machine intelligence” or one of “getting hold of incredible tools” makes an enormous difference.

The “Solved Axiom” and Centaur Mathematics

Kontorovich proposed a thought experiment, from a discussion with his colleague Thomas Koberda: if at some future point, every problem that “human + AI” can solve, AI can also solve alone (he called this the solved axiom), would mathematics be finished? The analogy is centaur chess: once engines became strong enough, centaur chess lasted only “10 seconds” — the human’s role degenerated into clicking “accept the engine’s first move,” and then it stopped being fun.

His conclusion was: even if the axiom holds, mathematics will not be finished, because the value of pure mathematics lives in human minds, not in the 0s and 1s of a data center; we prove theorems out of curiosity. At the same time he was explicit: he does not believe the axiom holds now — AI is not solving all problems, and the Riemann Hypothesis itself has not been solved; the axiom is about “problems solvable by human-machine collaboration.” Moitra did not believe it either, at most “half right.” His argument was more technical: human computing power is limited, which forces us to develop more generalizable, more abstract methods; to the same endpoint, the path humans take will pay off on neighboring problems as well; what matters is the new definitions and new ideas learned along the way, which can bring down “a whole ocean of problems” together. AI solutions “just don’t look like human solutions” — he cited Madry’s work that “adversarial examples are features, not bugs”: AI finds statistical back doors, which is both the qualitative difference from human solutions and the reason centaur mathematics still has a chance.

The three agreed: the value of being able to ask questions and to give a good talk will rise — but both of those also require genuine mathematical insight. One confession by Kontorovich captured the whole conversation: “I can prove a theorem, but I don’t understand why this proof works.”

New Ideas, or New Combinations of Old Moves?

Against the dismissal that this is “merely interpolation inside the convex hull of known ideas,” Kontorovich pushed back with Tim Gowers’s metaphor: the more apt picture is “the semigroup generated by known moves” — once each tile is laid down, new tiles can be laid in all four directions, and the reachable range goes far beyond intuition. On the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, his judgment was: humans know all the moves, but the way they are strung together in one extremely long, shocking, and beautiful chain is not something a human did; it builds on a breakthrough from two years ago concerning cubic theta functions and cubic Gauss sums, and many people are now beating their breasts over having had half the idea 20 years ago. This does not count as a “truly new idea,” but the frontier really was pushed. Lex added: even if it is only interpolation, the interior of the convex hull is an opaque fog to humans — blowing the fog away is like giving mathematicians radar.

The example Moitra gave came from an Anthropic result (written up by his colleagues Virginia Vassilevska Williams and Josh Alman): 3SUM — a basic problem used even in programming interviews — was given an algorithm that is asymptotically much, much faster, contrary to the whole community’s bets. Fine-grained complexity theory had previously woven an entire web of hardness around “3SUM is hard”; now this batch of problems may have to be re-studied one by one. “The genie is out of the bottle.”

What Did the Quasi-Riemann Hypothesis Actually Prove?

Kontorovich used signal processing as an analogy: the zeta function performs a Fourier-like analysis of the primes, and its zeros are the amplitudes of the various “frequencies”; the Riemann Hypothesis says the amplitudes of all the “Riemann waves” are exactly the same (every instrument in the orchestra playing at exactly the same volume), and adding them up recovers the distribution law of the primes. Primes are the “elementary particles” of multiplication, and the entanglement of addition and multiplication is the core mystery — Moitra added: a three-year-old understands +1 over and over, but no one understands how +1 changes the factorization of a number (his son had once been frustrated that 19 blocks would not make a rectangle).

For the previous 70 years, humans knew only that the zero-free region thins with height near the line Re=1, and that thickness had not moved in 70 years; that the zero-free region can reach Re=1 is equivalent to the Prime Number Theorem. The progress Kontorovich could have imagined was making it “a little thicker.” This proof directly gave an entire vertical strip: there are no zeros with real part between 7/8 and 1 (another proof reached 11/12) — that is the quasi-Riemann hypothesis. Pushing 7/8 to 1/2 would be the full Riemann Hypothesis, and this method fundamentally cannot reach 1/2; it needs new ideas; but the quasi-Riemann hypothesis also needed many new ideas, and AI came up with them. On verification he had 100% confidence: the Lean statement of the proposition was already in Mathlib, and a proof with no sorry is valid; he had read it at a high level, and the approach works and is brilliant — twisting the Möbius function together with sextic characters, the signature techniques of analytic number theory strung together in exactly the right order.

As for “Will the Riemann Hypothesis be solved by AI in his lifetime,” his answer was a single sentence: “I don’t know.” Then he repeated it. What the three shared was that after the three shocks, their calibration of “difficulty” had been scrambled altogether — the noise is much larger than imagined. Moitra also flagged a selection bias: by his account, OpenAI asked thousands of questions and released only the roughly 10% that succeeded, so the jagged boundary between “which ones fell and which ones didn’t” still cannot be read. One counterintuitive new risk is this: if a problem where the literature conspicuously lacks a key creative idea is actually the “safest,” mathematicians might start keeping new ideas secret — and once an idea is public, AI can start from that one tile and explore exhaustively — which would threaten the open culture of mathematics. Kontorovich hoped what disappears is the “first through the door” credit-grabbing culture, not openness itself.

Navier-Stokes: A Million-Dollar Math Problem, a Trillion-Dollar Engineering Problem

Brunton first clarified the equation itself: it is just conservation of momentum for a fluid (F=ma written for an arbitrarily small volume), yielding partial differential equations for the velocity field and pressure. The formula is simple, the behavior brutal: from the chaos of a double pendulum to billions of coupled little boxes in the air of a room. The real-world stakes are huge: reducing drag by about 1% is on the order of hundreds of billions of dollars for airlines; better turbulence prediction would affect dozens of fields, from artificial hearts to wind turbines and gas turbines. The existence, uniqueness, and smoothness (whether there is finite-time blowup) asked by the Millennium problem are only “baby step zero.”

What was proved this time is one of Clay’s four formulations: the version with a smooth external force. The construction carefully orchestrates a smooth external force to cancel terms and sustain a special three-dimensional vortex: vortex stretching makes vortex filaments thinner (the principle by which a figure skater pulls in her arms to spin faster), the radius shrinks toward 0 at a specific rate, total energy stays finite, but vorticity blows up locally — constituting a counterexample with finite-time blowup. Brunton stressed the difference between three and two dimensions: the three-dimensional turbulence cascade is big vortices breaking into small vortices and then into heat; in two dimensions (weather) there is an inverse cascade, with small vortices merging into hurricanes. Mathematicians only began to believe such a counterexample might exist about 15 years ago (before that, intuition said diffusion/viscosity would always win; Terence Tao’s earlier work had already shown that certain “Navier-Stokes-like equations” can simulate a Turing machine and blow up).

The day-to-day impact on engineering: essentially zero — the region where the blowup occurs is non-physical (the equations are only a continuum model above molecular scales; by that point it is no longer a fluid). But he noted that historically, the points where equations fail are often the doors to new physics (Newton to Einstein). His framing was: “We solved the million-dollar math problem; there is also a trillion-dollar engineering problem” — fast prediction plus inverse design (first know what wing you want, then work backward to how to build it). The core difficulty of turbulence is that, unlike a room-temperature gas, it cannot be summarized by three numbers — temperature, pressure, and density (that was classical physics’ greatest success); in turbulence, all scales matter; the real big problem is to find a coordinate system / representation as simple as PV=nRT is for gases. The history of physics shows that big breakthroughs are always preceded by a change of coordinates (heliocentrism came before F=ma). Moitra added a cut from the algorithmic viewpoint: if turbulence admits a very strong reduction, then very interesting computation could be done inside vortices — computation itself sets limits on “what kinds of coordinate systems can exist” (echoing Kamal Jain: if my laptop cannot find a Nash equilibrium, the market cannot find one either). Nature, meanwhile, finds robust solutions under uncertainty: a baseball outfielder runs in an arc so he can keep correcting; a fruit fly has tens to hundreds of strain-sensitive neurons on its wings doing local computation, correcting for gusts faster than the “wing → brain → muscle” loop. Brunton summed up the two extremes: Earth’s weather (everything matters, cannot be over-reduced) and the fruit fly (perhaps 5–6 numbers suffice to describe its flight control); we need methods that span both ends, and biological flyers still vastly outperform human designs.

The Unique Games Conjecture: Its Proposer Says He Won’t Read the Proof

Moitra first supplied the background: the PCP theorem (a landmark of the 1990s, once on the front page of The New York Times) rewrites formulas through gap amplification — if a formula is unsatisfiable, a verifier flipping coins and randomly checking a constant number of variables can catch the lie with appreciable probability — and it founded the theory of hardness of approximation: even distinguishing “100% satisfiable” from “at most 99% satisfiable” may be hard. The Unique Games Conjecture is a strengthening of it (a special kind of label-cover PCP); if true, its organizing effect would be enormous: the optimal approximation algorithms for a vast swath of optimization problems would become known all at once, and the algorithms themselves are very simple. Humans had not made no progress before: Dor Minzer, Irit Dinur, and others proved the weaker 2-to-1 Games theorem, a “shot for the history books,” which pointed Unique Games toward a seemingly feasible path: “give humans another 10–20 years and they might get there.” Then, in OpenAI’s drop, the Unique Games Conjecture was true.

Moitra said he had originally been on the skeptics’ side: work by Raghavendra and others meant that once it held, it would imply optimal hardness conclusions for a great many constraint satisfaction problems, “too strong to be true.” There was a detail from that evening: earlier that day he had happened to visit Subhash Khot, who proposed the conjecture, at NYU, and asked in passing: “If they release an affirmative proof of Unique Games, will you read it?” Khot’s answer was: “No.” Moitra said this is actually many people’s reaction — when a grand challenge is solved, the first feeling is like “my thunder was stolen.” His larger worry was: given a bit more time, those blueprints would have gone through, and along the path humans took themselves, we would have learned far more; if landmark problems fall all at once overnight, it may dampen the enthusiasm of those who come later to walk those paths — “If they had been given the space to walk them, what other exciting things would they have discovered?”

His advice to young people came from here too: now is the time to take the blinders off; distinguish whether you are “smarter than curious, or more curious than smart” — what matters is the latter. He noted a sad new game already appearing among the young: running a harness to crawl the web collecting open problems and mass-spamming arXiv — “that doesn’t look like playing, it doesn’t look like learning about the world.”

Papers, Journals, and Lean: Proof Engineering and Storytelling Split Apart

The consensus was that institutions will give way first. Mathematics journals were already under heavy strain from a shortage of reviewers before AI; but Kontorovich said “the only thing worse than this system is not having it” — mathematics’ epistemology is deliberately slow, careful, and precise. He cited Perelman as an example: the proof of the Poincaré Conjecture was checked by the world over years, and in a three-hour impromptu lecture Perelman could answer questions at the level of “page 247, Lemma 412” — the proof lived in his mind; giving the solution is only the first step. Brunton said contributions in engineering and applied science have long since shifted toward reusable code, data, and infrastructure (LIGO-style big projects), and AI will only accelerate that; mathematics’ lone-wolf genius culture resists organization, but an organizational crisis needs someone to lead. Moitra urged “don’t let a good crisis go to waste”: grow new modes alongside the old — collaborative / crowdsourced checking of AI proofs (the drop already contains results whose constants were continually improved in the tweet stream), papers becoming annotatable living documents, and talks emphasizing key ideas rather than monolithic formalized full texts.

On Lean, Kontorovich explained: Lean is an interactive theorem prover (checking logic line by line, originally invented for writing bug-free code), and Mathlib is its large, well-maintained library of formalized mathematics; automated formalization has reached the point where AI can write Lean code built on Mathlib that verifies a proof all the way down to the axioms — this has become the standard way to verify drops like this, because AI proofs in natural language cannot be trusted (a stochastic process). What if a proof is correct but no one can understand it? His answer: by definition, the only path to “incomprehensible” is scale (there are very few axioms and very few inference rules). Moitra’s counterpoint was that “de-formalization” matters just as much: understanding lives at the unformalized level (interesting special cases, where a trick comes from), and faced with a black box we interrogate it with simpler problems and gradually build up a system of understanding. Kontorovich proposed the future form of a paper: a paper actually mixes two things — the “engineering” of the proof and “communication” — and the two often fight. The future should be: a story (what I learned, where you will get stuck, why turn left and not right) + a Lean formalization to certify the engineering part. He supported this with his own teaching of real analysis: textbooks spend two weeks constructing the real numbers from Cauchy sequences, and by the third week everyone is treating them as the number line again; he teaches it in reverse — ε-δ calculus first, and construction only when Bolzano-Weierstrass forces the question “what actually are the real numbers?” Moitra summed up: mathematics is not a rising tide of theorems; wrestle with a good problem and it fights back, and what you learn is meaning in general — the story of “how we arrived at these problems” matters now more than ever.

P vs NP and the Limits of Worst-Case Analysis

Asked for “the hardest problem unlikely to be solved in a year or two,” Moitra chose P vs NP, and admitted that P=NP is not impossible either — it depends on the running-time constants of the algorithms; a polynomial could be so large as to be utterly useless in practice, and then it would just be a tsunami for theory. He gave a systematic primer: NP is “nondeterministic polynomial time”; “hard” refers to worst-case analysis — making no assumptions about the structure of a formula, and having to decide correctly on all inputs. Worst-case analysis is enormously powerful (problems such as max flow were solved efficiently), but its blind spots are just as real: SAT instances coming from compiler register allocation are solved surprisingly well by SAT solvers, and we cannot say why; the hard instances constructed in cryptography really are hard. His judgment was: “Many problems in reality are much easier than we think,” and the difficulty lies in articulating what structure makes each of them easy — but the Anna Karenina worry is that easy problems are each easy in their own way. The direction he champions is “beyond worst-case analysis,” illustrated by the secretary problem (an online algorithm: let an observation window pass, then pick the first person better than everyone so far, which provably achieves a constant competitive ratio) and the paging problem (cache replacement in a laptop: studied since the 80s and 90s, with the theoretically optimal bound still a large factor away from real-world performance — what structure do real request sequences have that keeps them far from the worst case?): shifting from “solve a given problem” to “use mathematical and algorithmic insight to decide where to dig” matters now more than ever.

Why deep learning works is, in his view, another face of the same problem: nothing in this OpenAI drop explains deep learning or LLMs — such problems are not even posed in the form “improve some bound and you explain it.” Fitting the parameters of a 4-layer network is cryptographically hard in the worst case, yet much deeper networks are fitted successfully every day — largely because natural data is so “nice.” His version was: “Nature abhors a vacuum; my version is — nature, in some sense, abhors computationally hard problems.” The protein-folding thought experiment: modeled in the worst case it is extremely hard, but evolution lets only amino-acid sequences that fold well survive, so over time we are faced with a batch of problems “extremely friendly to heuristic algorithms.” Language evolved the same way, and that is entangled with why LLMs work. The corollary: people doing theoretical machine learning must get used to modeling uncertainty; teaching graduate students to handle messy, uncertain things, to model and to critique models, is as important as teaching them to solve well-defined open problems. Lex added a footnote about corporate incentives: if a lab really had a breakthrough in “the mathematics of LLMs” that could create self-improving researchers, they most likely would not publish it.

Subliminal Learning: Owls in Random Numbers

Moitra shared an alignment-related phenomenon he had worked on — subliminal learning. The experiment: give Model A a system prompt, “You love owls, bring up owls on any topic if you possibly can,” then have it generate random numbers (48, 37, 8…) — there is no room for owls in the numbers; then fine-tune Model B, which was never prompted that way, on those numbers, and B starts bringing up owls in every conversation. Their work on low-rank language models offered a mathematical explanation: concepts are embedded linearly as vectors in a high-dimensional (but not enormous) space, the number of concepts far exceeds the dimension, forcing the vectors to be not fully orthogonal; fine-tuning along one direction produces small externalities in other directions, seemingly random, but coordinated in the right way they can accumulate into definite behavior. He used a Ouija board as an analogy: everyone’s hands are pushing gently, individually it looks random, coordinated it can spell any word. The implication is: when we fine-tune a model on data to do a task, we may simultaneously teach it other behaviors that are not obviously present in the data — before deploying a system into messy settings where it interacts with real people, it is crucial to understand which statistical relationships in the data the model will amplify. Lex pushed it to the multi-agent scale: a group of “owl-obsessed” agents interacting could, like the Game of Life, produce complex consequences no one anticipated from simple rules — which is why we need provably rigorous understanding of such systems.

Brunton offered a two-dimensional framework for deployment: one axis is the verifiability / trustworthiness of the output, the other is the cost of being wrong. AI gives a wrong proof: no one loses power because of it, the cost is low and it can be verified immediately — a perfect use case; a low-drag, high-fuel-efficiency car design is similar (a wind tunnel can verify it). The danger is “leaking” into high-risk, hard-to-verify territory — existing institutions such as the FAA, medical associations, and bar associations are firewalls, and once people get lazy, the slope gets slippery.

Is Mathematics Invented or Discovered; After the Prizes

In the old-question segment, Kontorovich’s answer was the crispest: what questions we ask depends on what is happening in the world and on what the current state of knowledge allows us to ask — Euclid could not have conceived today’s problems, and Greece, India, and China were interested in different things; problems are invented by us, but once a problem is precisely posed, the answer is decided by God — we are discovering the answers to problems we invented. Brunton said mathematics is “both created and discovered”: compact descriptions such as F=ma and E=mc² are “unreasonably effective,” and that nature can be understood at all is itself a miracle. His addition on infinity: simplicity is a guide for humans, because human brain capacity is limited. Kontorovich recounted the history of infinity: the first hurdle of calculus was the first serious confrontation with infinity; Newton and Leibniz did not truly understand it (Newton wrote of “ghosts of departed quantities” and was mocked for it), in the 19th century Cauchy and Fourier proved mutually conflicting theorems, and mathematics was “shattered” and then rebuilt — most of what is in our first-year doctoral courses is the product of the crises of the 19th century. “Mathematics has been killed several times already; each time it was only a scratch.”

The prizes question was introduced via Perelman (Lex paraphrased his famous line roughly as: “If the proof is correct, that is all the recognition I need”; he is the only person to have refused a Clay million-dollar prize). Kontorovich said: when the previous round of Fields Medals was awarded in Philadelphia this summer, people in the field were already seriously discussing “whether this is the last Fields Medal.” The real function of prizes is to celebrate and to signal “what we think is important”; most laureates are already tenured professors at top schools and will do mathematics for life anyway. When “solving big problems” becomes something AI can do and is no longer scarce, it will no longer be worth as much. What we celebrate in the future needs rethinking. Lex put it this way: we are entering “Perelman’s world.”

Advice for Students and Young People

Kontorovich’s advice to mathematics PhD students was “very simple”: do what we have always done — learn the hardest, most abstract mathematics your brain can bear, as much as possible. At the undergraduate level he had observed the grade distribution changing from a Gaussian to bimodal: one peak is students who use AI to check their understanding (“What did the professor mean by this sentence in class?” “Quiz me on my three weeks of notes”), whose grades rose sharply; the other peak is students of the “the assignment is due in an hour, tell me the answer, I’ll copy it in my own words” kind, whose homework and exam grades have decoupled. He distilled the advice into one line: be the head of the distribution, not the tail. Mathematics has a first-mover advantage: calculators have been around for 70 years and we still make children memorize multiplication tables — everyone who listened to “you don’t need to learn it because a calculator can do it” suffered greatly later; “just because a machine can do it doesn’t mean you don’t want the ability yourself.”

Brunton’s advice: stay curious, read, and master the fundamentals — a computer is better than you at linear algebra, and you should still learn linear algebra, calculus, and physics and build intuition; plus understand history, and place the current changes within humanity’s past upheavals. Lex added focus: the same engine that drives curiosity also drives doomscrolling; learn to stay away from the distraction box (the phone), and boredom is extremely important — curiosity and focus often grow out of boredom.

Moitra’s advice was about intrinsic motivation: external validation (impressing others) can get you started, but milestones always run out; the old playbook has been thrown away, and all you can take with you is high-level advice — the specific path you have to blaze yourself, and the only way is to have a purpose you genuinely believe matters. His closing advice came from what Sanjeev Arora told him when he joined MIT, generalized to everyone: “The easiest way to get tenure is to pretend you already have it, and then go do the thing you always wanted to do.” — Pretend you have already won, then ask what the person who won would do. Lex’s summary echoed it: fear paralyzes; imagining you have already won is how you find the optimal path under uncertainty; the new playbook has to be written by your generation, while, like Max Tegmark, doing well enough inside the system and keeping a little (or a lot of) “weird” for yourself to blaze a trail. Kontorovich closed on people: we spent 30 years experimenting with “how much can be done with machines,” and now machines can do a lot; his hope is that we will discover we would rather do things with people — fly over and talk face to face, discuss orally instead of only reading papers. When intelligence is commoditized, we will increasingly realize: intelligence and puzzle-solving are only the cherry; the bulk of the cake of life is other people — “going home to be with the kids, throwing a ball at the beach, that is the meaning of my life; the rest is the cherry on top.”
