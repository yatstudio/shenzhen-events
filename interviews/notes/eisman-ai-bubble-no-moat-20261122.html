大卖空艾斯曼：AI 砸了几千亿，却没有护城河

来源：YouTube 频道「股市基友」转载的 Steve Eisman 播客专访中字版（2026-11-22 发布，44 分钟） 嘉宾：Steve Eisman——2008 年做空次贷成名、《大卖空》一书中 Steve Carell 所演角色的原型，现运营自己的播客《The Real Eisman Playbook》 本纪要据视频自带繁体中文字幕整理；文中数字与判断均为嘉宾自述口径（部分引其转述的财报与媒体数据），未另行核实。

一句话

艾斯曼说他不预测泡沫何时破，但他把 AI 产业链的风险画成了一条链：从英伟达到云巨头，所有人的收入最后都押在 Anthropic 和 OpenAI 两家巨亏的公司身上；而这两家公司最大的问题是——这个行业砸了几千亿美元，至今没有护城河。

一、集中度风险：整条链只有两个客户

  • 他先讲了一个分析框架：如果一家成熟软件公司有几千个客户，那很好；如果只有两个客户，你不会投，因为任何一个出事公司就完了——「整个 AI 的故事现在就是这种情况」。
  • 英伟达：他本人持有，财报营收同比增约 110%（地球上最大的公司营收翻倍，他说这本身就疯狂）；但翻开当晚公布的 10-Q 附注七：截至 7 月底，英伟达 70% 的应收账款只来自五个客户。
  • 云巨头：微软、亚马逊、Google 的 AI 收入中约 70% 只来自 Anthropic 和 OpenAI 两家，而这部分 AI 收入约占它们云总收入的 25%–30%。
  • 甲骨文是最极端的例子：它的剩余履约义务（RPO，未交付订单）去年一季之内从约 1500 亿美元暴涨到 4000 亿，如今已超 6000 亿，其中约 50% 来自 OpenAI 一家——「甲骨文一半的未来收入，来自一家疯狂亏钱的公司」。甲骨文股价曾两天内从 230 涨到 330 美元，之后几个月跌到 150 附近；标普已把它的债信评级降到 BBB-，只比垃圾级高一级。
  • 链条最底端的两家并不健康（他引《华尔街日报》数据）：OpenAI 今年二季度营收约 65 亿美元、成本约 120 亿；一个季度内营收增加 10 亿、成本却增加 30 亿，营收环比增 18%；Anthropic 营收 110 亿美元以上、同季增超 100%，相对强些，但两家都在「大量失血」。
  • 结论：承诺是这两家下的，整条链都押在它们未来的成长上。它们若有一家失败、或者金主们决定不再掏钱，链就会慢下来——按他的说法，「假设 OpenAI 倒了，整个美国会一夜之间陷入衰退」。未来一年左右，这两家公司必须保持健康。

二、钱从哪来：循环融资的味道

  • 他原以为资金主要来自创投，实际不是：大头是亚马逊、Google、微软、英伟达的股权投资，加上软银——这些人已经掏了钱，还想不想继续掏，他不知道。
  • 主持人问这像不像《大卖空》里的 CDO 套娃（CDO A 的一部分放进 CDO B，再放进 CDO C），他承认「有些相似之处」：融资确实有循环的性质。但他也给对方留了话：只要两家实验室持续飞快成长、金主继续给钱，这条链就撑得住；风险在于如果 AI 收入的集中度不是 70% 而是 10%，「我们现在谈的就会完全不一样」。

三、没有护城河：这个行业真正的商业弱点

  • 「狂刷 token」的时代结束了：去年两家实验室收费太低把自己拖得够呛，后来涨价；企业这边，Uber 三四个月就烧光全年的 AI 预算，另一家公司花了 5 亿美元还不知道花在哪——大家开始有成本意识。结果是：只有最重要的任务才用 Anthropic、OpenAI 的模型，其余都换成 Kimi K3 这类开源权重模型，「不是每件事都需要凯迪拉克」。
  • 他反复说的那句话是：「这个产业尽管已经砸了好几千亿美元，还是没有护城河。」对照组是当年的 Google 搜索——全球九成人想都不想就用它，那才叫护城河；而今天用户在 Gemini、ChatGPT、Claude 之间来回轮换，毫无忠诚度；开发者用着 Claude，明天有更好的模型立刻就换，没有任何锁定。
  • 云巨头则是有两面性：盖数据中心要 1000 亿美元，规模本身是护城河；但它们的云业务偏偏依赖这些没有护城河的大模型公司，这是它们现在的弱点。
  • 他的「阴谋论」：所谓「AI 会毁灭世界」的说法是障眼法。实验室们真正怕的是狂刷 token 结束、没有护城河、中国开源模型抢份额，于是制造恐慌、推动政府监管——监管会创造护城河：一纸禁令不准用中国模型，法律壁垒立刻就有了，还能顺势形成双头垄断。他的证据很简单：如果 Dario 真心认为自家产品危险到需要放慢，他就应该推迟 IPO——但他没有。马斯克在 X 上的调侃（大意）是：你的产品会毁灭世界？顺便问一句，IPO 能分我多少？

四、云巨头的现金流去哪了

  • 三四年前，微软、亚马逊、Google 赚钱多到现金不知道怎么花，只能回购股票；现在因为天文数字的数据中心资本开支，自由现金流没了，有的甚至是负的。
  • 最让他震惊的是 Google 募了 850 亿美元股权资金——一家上市以来从没募过资、躺着就能生出现金的公司去增发股票，「一两年前你跟我说这个，我会说你疯了」。主持人给的数字是：光云巨头今年的 AI 资本支出就约 7000 亿美元。
  • Michael Burry 的折旧论点（云巨头去年 11 月把芯片折旧年限从 3–4 年拉长到 5–6 年，等于按个开关就增厚利润；且新芯片太多、旧芯片过时太快），艾斯曼评价是「太学术了」：现在需求大到连旧芯片都在涨价；如果两家实验室成长暴冲，折旧年限根本不重要；如果 OpenAI 失败、整条链反转，修正也会发生，跟折旧无关——影响大局的因素比折旧大得多。

五、电力：美国的真瓶颈

  • 他引马斯克对《经济学人》的说法：中国有芯片问题，美国有电力问题——而且中国能解决芯片问题，电力是实体基础设施，要花很长时间，没人能确定它会卡到什么程度（「排队吧，没人知道」）。
  • 美国用电量 15 年来首次恢复增长，年增 3%–4%，以美国的基数等于每年多出两座大城市的需求。GE Vernova 是他口中的典型受益者：几年前 GE 分拆时，卖方分析师给这块能源业务的估值是负 30 亿美元；如今它的订单排到了 2035 年，上季积压订单增约 85%。数据中心主要靠燃气轮机供电（涡轮机和喷气发动机同源，一台有五间房那么大、要几年才造得出来）；马斯克在孟菲斯建 Colossus 时电网跟不上，最后买了 35 台移动式燃气轮机自己发电。Bloom Energy 这类自备小型发电的公司是替代方案。

六、如果非要买 AI：买收钱的人，不买烧钱的人

  • 他明确不买大模型公司（没有护城河），对云巨头保持警戒（现金流全没了）；他的打法是持有「拿到那些现金流的公司」：英伟达、美光、GE Vernova、Arista Networks、思科，工业侧还有做电气化的伊顿——「卖铲子，不是炫目的软件」。
  • 软件业里他区分两种命运：被 agent 架空的中间层危险——他举 Meta 的 agent 产品 Muse 为例，你让它订机票订酒店，它跑遍所有旅游网站找最低价，于是 Booking、Travelocity 不再拥有客户，「侧栏广告对 AI 没用」，支付领域可能同样危险；而深深嵌入企业流程的软件（Salesforce、ServiceNow 这类换掉极其痛苦的）大概没事，看的正是转换门槛有多稳。
  • Palantir 他很想持有，但现价太贵不会买——「有故事，但理性」。
  • 和 1999 年像不像？架构确实类似（新科技＋金融投机），但现在下结论太早：如果 OpenAI 或 Anthropic 倒了，会有真正的修正，然后下一代会冒出来收拾残局；会不会发生，他说他不知道。

七、他的操盘法与问答环节

  • 方法论：先看故事、不是价值投资（「股票便宜通常是有原因的」），但绝不乱花钱买贵货；喜欢护城河（因此持有穆迪、Visa），但不是硬性要求；最讨厌管理层卖股票；一般不碰周期股（那等于只是在预测经济）；喜欢有真实故事和真实成长顺风的公司。他自称是线性思考者，写作按 1、2、3、4、5 到结论；他太太 Valerie 负责编辑他周五的每周总结，总把顺序倒过来——因为大多数人看不到最后，结论不能埋在最底下。
  • 美债（观众问题）：债务 40 万亿美元，平均利率从 2020 年的 1.77% 升到 3.45%，年利息支出从 5230 亿升到 1.22 万亿美元。他对债务螺旋论怀疑：美元是全球储备货币，美国公债是全球金融体系的根基（全球银行用它做隔夜回购拆借），只要没有替代品，他就不太担心，尽管他希望赤字小一点。要盯的数字是 10 年期收益率而不是联邦基金利率：5% 是市场的分水岭（他原以为 4.5%，承认猜错了）；录制当天约 4.98%，前一天曾短暂冲破 5%。贝森特的回购计划真实目的是压低长端利率、减轻利息负担，但宣布的 60 亿美元面对 40 万亿债务毫无用处，「到目前为止他惨败了」，他猜贝森特还会出别的招。
  • 贵金属与比特币：他从没买过贵金属，观点近巴菲特——放在那儿闪闪发亮、什么都不生产，有价值只是因为大家说它有价值。比特币在他眼里更糟：它的走势和自己的论点相反——按信徒的逻辑，通胀担忧、利率上升、股市下跌的日子它该涨，纳斯达克狂涨、利率下降的日子它该跌，实际却恰好反过来；「它似乎就只是投机资产，一种对投机做投机的方式」。
  • 投资地域：他做对冲基金时投过海外，发现完全没有日夜分散的效果，多年来只做美股，「美国就够我忙了」。
  • 收尾被问到有没有某个数字会成为引爆点（让美国被迫砍福利或大幅加息），他的回答回到了政治：债务问题纯粹是政治问题，华盛顿现在两党都没人愿意把赤字砍掉哪怕一美元。

大賣空艾斯曼：AI 砸了幾千億，卻沒有護城河

來源：YouTube 頻道「股市基友」轉載的 Steve Eisman 播客專訪中字版（2026-11-22 發布，44 分鐘） 嘉賓：Steve Eisman——2008 年做空次貸成名、《大賣空》一書中 Steve Carell 所演角色的原型，現運營自己的播客《The Real Eisman Playbook》 本紀要據影片內建繁體中文字幕整理；文中數字與判斷均為嘉賓自述口徑（部分引其轉述的財報與媒體數據），未另行核實。

一句話

艾斯曼說他不預測泡沫何時破，但他把 AI 產業鏈的風險畫成了一條鏈：從英偉達到雲端巨頭，所有人的收入最後都押在 Anthropic 和 OpenAI 兩家巨虧的公司身上；而這兩家公司最大的問題是——這個行業砸了幾千億美元，至今沒有護城河。

一、集中度風險：整條鏈只有兩個客戶

  • 他先講了一個分析框架：如果一家成熟軟體公司有幾千個客戶，那很好；如果只有兩個客戶，你不會投，因為任何一個出事公司就完了——「整個 AI 的故事現在就是這種情況」。
  • 英偉達：他本人持有，財報營收年增約 110%（地球上最大的公司營收翻倍，他說這本身就瘋狂）；但翻開當晚公布的 10-Q 附註七：截至 7 月底，英偉達 70% 的應收帳款只來自五個客戶。
  • 雲端巨頭：微軟、亞馬遜、Google 的 AI 收入中約 70% 只來自 Anthropic 和 OpenAI 兩家，而這部分 AI 收入約占它們雲端總收入的 25%–30%。
  • 甲骨文是最極端的例子：它的剩餘履約義務（RPO，未交付訂單）去年一季之內從約 1500 億美元暴漲到 4000 億，如今已超 6000 億，其中約 50% 來自 OpenAI 一家——「甲骨文一半的未來收入，來自一家瘋狂虧錢的公司」。甲骨文股價曾兩天內從 230 漲到 330 美元，之後幾個月跌到 150 附近；標普已把它的債信評級降到 BBB-，只比垃圾級高一級。
  • 鏈條最底端的兩家並不健康（他引《華爾街日報》數據）：OpenAI 今年二季度營收約 65 億美元、成本約 120 億；一個季度內營收增加 10 億、成本卻增加 30 億，營收季增 18%；Anthropic 營收 110 億美元以上、同季增超 100%，相對強些，但兩家都在「大量失血」。
  • 結論：承諾是這兩家下的，整條鏈都押在它們未來的成長上。它們若有一家失敗、或者金主們決定不再掏錢，鏈就會慢下來——按他的說法，「假設 OpenAI 倒了，整個美國會一夜之間陷入衰退」。未來一年左右，這兩家公司必須保持健康。

二、錢從哪來：循環融資的味道

  • 他原以為資金主要來自創投，實際不是：大頭是亞馬遜、Google、微軟、英偉達的股權投資，加上軟銀——這些人已經掏了錢，還想不想繼續掏，他不知道。
  • 主持人問這像不像《大賣空》裡的 CDO 套娃（CDO A 的一部分放進 CDO B，再放進 CDO C），他承認「有些相似之處」：融資確實有循環的性質。但他也給對方留了話：只要兩家實驗室持續飛快成長、金主繼續給錢，這條鏈就撐得住；風險在於如果 AI 收入的集中度不是 70% 而是 10%，「我們現在談的就會完全不一樣」。

三、沒有護城河：這個行業真正的商業弱點

  • 「狂刷 token」的時代結束了：去年兩家實驗室收費太低把自己拖得夠嗆，後來漲價；企業這邊，Uber 三四個月就燒光全年的 AI 預算，另一家公司花了 5 億美元還不知道花在哪——大家開始有成本意識。結果是：只有最重要的任務才用 Anthropic、OpenAI 的模型，其餘都換成 Kimi K3 這類開源權重模型，「不是每件事都需要凱迪拉克」。
  • 他反覆說的那句話是：「這個產業儘管已經砸了好幾千億美元，還是沒有護城河。」對照組是當年的 Google 搜尋——全球九成人想都不想就用它，那才叫護城河；而今天使用者在 Gemini、ChatGPT、Claude 之間來回輪換，毫無忠誠度；開發者用著 Claude，明天有更好的模型立刻就換，沒有任何鎖定。
  • 雲端巨頭則是有兩面性：蓋資料中心要 1000 億美元，規模本身是護城河；但它們的雲端業務偏偏依賴這些沒有護城河的大模型公司，這是它們現在的弱點。
  • 他的「陰謀論」：所謂「AI 會毀滅世界」的說法是障眼法。實驗室們真正怕的是狂刷 token 結束、沒有護城河、中國開源模型搶市占，於是製造恐慌、推動政府監管——監管會創造護城河：一紙禁令不准用中國模型，法律壁壘立刻就有了，還能順勢形成雙頭壟斷。他的證據很簡單：如果 Dario 真心認為自家產品危險到需要放慢，他就應該推遲 IPO——但他沒有。馬斯克在 X 上的調侃（大意）是：你的產品會毀滅世界？順便問一句，IPO 能分我多少？

四、雲端巨頭的現金流去哪了

  • 三四年前，微軟、亞馬遜、Google 賺錢多到現金不知道怎麼花，只能回購股票；現在因為天文數字的資料中心資本支出，自由現金流沒了，有的甚至是負的。
  • 最讓他震驚的是 Google 募了 850 億美元股權資金——一家上市以來從沒募過資、躺著就能生出現金的公司去增發股票，「一兩年前你跟我說這個，我會說你瘋了」。主持人給的數字是：光雲端巨頭今年的 AI 資本支出就約 7000 億美元。
  • Michael Burry 的折舊論點（雲端巨頭去年 11 月把晶片折舊年限從 3–4 年拉長到 5–6 年，等於按個開關就增厚利潤；且新晶片太多、舊晶片過時太快），艾斯曼評價是「太學術了」：現在需求大到連舊晶片都在漲價；如果兩家實驗室成長暴衝，折舊年限根本不重要；如果 OpenAI 失敗、整條鏈反轉，修正也會發生，跟折舊無關——影響大局的因素比折舊大得多。

五、電力：美國的真瓶頸

  • 他引馬斯克對《經濟學人》的說法：中國有晶片問題，美國有電力問題——而且中國能解決晶片問題，電力是實體基礎設施，要花很長時間，沒人能確定它會卡到什麼程度（「排隊吧，沒人知道」）。
  • 美國用電量 15 年來首次恢復成長，年增 3%–4%，以美國的基數等於每年多出兩座大城市的需求。GE Vernova 是他口中的典型受益者：幾年前 GE 分拆時，賣方分析師給這塊能源業務的估值是負 30 億美元；如今它的訂單排到了 2035 年，上季積壓訂單增約 85%。資料中心主要靠燃氣渦輪機供電（渦輪機和噴射引擎同源，一台有五間房那麼大、要幾年才造得出來）；馬斯克在孟菲斯建 Colossus 時電網跟不上，最後買了 35 台移動式燃氣渦輪機自己發電。Bloom Energy 這類自備小型發電的公司是替代方案。

六、如果非要買 AI：買收錢的人，不買燒錢的人

  • 他明確不買大模型公司（沒有護城河），對雲端巨頭保持警戒（現金流全沒了）；他的打法是持有「拿到那些現金流的公司」：英偉達、美光、GE Vernova、Arista Networks、思科，工業側還有做電氣化的伊頓——「賣鏟子，不是炫目的軟體」。
  • 軟體業裡他區分兩種命運：被 agent 架空的中間層危險——他舉 Meta 的 agent 產品 Muse 為例，你讓它訂機票訂酒店，它跑遍所有旅遊網站找最低價，於是 Booking、Travelocity 不再擁有客戶，「側欄廣告對 AI 沒用」，支付領域可能同樣危險；而深深嵌入企業流程的軟體（Salesforce、ServiceNow 這類換掉極其痛苦的）大概沒事，看的正是轉換門檻有多穩。
  • Palantir 他很想持有，但現價太貴不會買——「有故事，但理性」。
  • 和 1999 年像不像？架構確實類似（新科技＋金融投機），但現在下結論太早：如果 OpenAI 或 Anthropic 倒了，會有真正的修正，然後下一代會冒出來收拾殘局；會不會發生，他說他不知道。

七、他的操盤法與問答環節

  • 方法論：先看故事、不是價值投資（「股票便宜通常是有原因的」），但絕不亂花錢買貴貨；喜歡護城河（因此持有穆迪、Visa），但不是硬性要求；最討厭管理層賣股票；一般不碰週期股（那等於只是在預測經濟）；喜歡有真實故事和真實成長順風的公司。他自稱是線性思考者，寫作按 1、2、3、4、5 到結論；他太太 Valerie 負責編輯他週五的每週總結，總把順序倒過來——因為大多數人看不到最後，結論不能埋在最底下。
  • 美債（觀眾問題）：債務 40 兆美元，平均利率從 2020 年的 1.77% 升到 3.45%，年利息支出從 5230 億升到 1.22 兆美元。他對債務螺旋論懷疑：美元是全球儲備貨幣，美國公債是全球金融體系的根基（全球銀行用它做隔夜附買回拆借），只要沒有替代品，他就不太擔心，儘管他希望赤字小一點。要盯的數字是 10 年期殖利率而不是聯邦基金利率：5% 是市場的分水嶺（他原以為 4.5%，承認猜錯了）；錄製當天約 4.98%，前一天曾短暫衝破 5%。貝森特的回購計畫真實目的是壓低長端利率、減輕利息負擔，但宣布的 60 億美元面對 40 兆債務毫無用處，「到目前為止他慘敗了」，他猜貝森特還會出別的招。
  • 貴金屬與比特幣：他從沒買過貴金屬，觀點近巴菲特——放在那兒閃閃發亮、什麼都不生產，有價值只是因為大家說它有價值。比特幣在他眼裡更糟：它的走勢和自己的論點相反——按信徒的邏輯，通膨擔憂、利率上升、股市下跌的日子它該漲，那斯達克狂漲、利率下降的日子它該跌，實際卻恰好反過來；「它似乎就只是投機資產，一種對投機做投機的方式」。
  • 投資地域：他做對沖基金時投過海外，發現完全沒有日夜分散的效果，多年來只做美股，「美國就夠我忙了」。
  • 收尾被問到有沒有某個數字會成為引爆點（讓美國被迫砍福利或大幅升息），他的回答回到了政治：債務問題純粹是政治問題，華盛頓現在兩黨都沒人願意把赤字砍掉哪怕一美元。

Big Short Eisman: AI Has Spent Hundreds of Billions — and Still Has No Moat

Source: A Chinese-subtitled rebroadcast, by the YouTube channel 「股市基友」, of a podcast interview with Steve Eisman (released 2026-11-22, 44 minutes) Guest: Steve Eisman — who became famous shorting subprime mortgages in 2008 and is the real-life basis for the character played by Steve Carell in the book *The Big Short*; he now runs his own podcast, *The Real Eisman Playbook* These notes are compiled from the Traditional Chinese subtitles provided with the video; all figures and judgments in the text are as stated by the guest (some citing financial reports and media data he relayed) and have not been independently verified.

The one-liner

Eisman says he doesn't predict when the bubble will burst, but he draws the risk in the AI supply chain as a single chain: from NVIDIA to the cloud giants, everyone's revenue ultimately rests on two deeply loss-making companies, Anthropic and OpenAI — and the biggest problem with those two is that, after hundreds of billions of dollars have been spent, the industry still has no moat.

1. Concentration risk: the whole chain has only two customers

  • He starts with an analytical framework: if a mature software company has thousands of customers, that's great; if it has only two customers, you wouldn't invest, because if either one runs into trouble the company is finished — "that is exactly what the whole AI story is right now."
  • NVIDIA: he holds it himself; its reported revenue grew about 110% year over year (the largest company on Earth doubling its revenue — he says that in itself is insane); but open Note 7 of the 10-Q released that evening: as of the end of July, 70% of NVIDIA's accounts receivable came from just five customers.
  • The cloud giants: about 70% of the AI revenue at Microsoft, Amazon and Google comes from just two companies, Anthropic and OpenAI, and that AI revenue accounts for roughly 25%–30% of their total cloud revenue.
  • Oracle is the most extreme example: its remaining performance obligations (RPO, undelivered orders) ballooned from about $150 billion to $400 billion within a single quarter last year and now exceed $600 billion, of which roughly 50% comes from OpenAI alone — "half of Oracle's future revenue comes from a company that is losing money like crazy." Oracle's stock once rose from $230 to $330 in two days, then fell to around $150 over the following months; S&P has downgraded its credit rating to BBB-, just one notch above junk.
  • The two companies at the bottom of the chain are not healthy (he cites *Wall Street Journal* figures): OpenAI's revenue in the second quarter of this year was about $6.5 billion against costs of about $12 billion; within one quarter revenue grew by $1 billion while costs grew by $3 billion, with revenue up 18% quarter over quarter; Anthropic's revenue was above $11 billion, up more than 100% in the same quarter — relatively stronger, but both are "bleeding heavily."
  • The conclusion: the commitments were made by these two companies, and the whole chain is bet on their future growth. If one of them fails, or their backers decide to stop writing checks, the chain slows down — in his words, "if OpenAI goes down, the whole United States falls into recession overnight." For roughly the next year, these two companies have to stay healthy.

2. Where the money comes from: a whiff of circular financing

  • He had assumed the funding came mainly from venture capital; it doesn't: the bulk is equity investment from Amazon, Google, Microsoft and NVIDIA, plus SoftBank — these parties have already put money in, and whether they want to keep putting money in, he doesn't know.
  • Asked by the host whether this resembles the CDOs-within-CDOs of *The Big Short* (part of CDO A goes into CDO B, then into CDO C), he concedes there are "some similarities": the financing does have a circular quality. But he also gives the other side its due: as long as the two labs keep growing fast and the backers keep funding them, the chain holds; the risk is that if the concentration of AI revenue were 10% instead of 70%, "we would be having a completely different conversation right now."

3. No moat: the industry's real business weakness

  • The era of "burning tokens like crazy" is over: last year the two labs priced too low and hurt themselves badly, then raised prices; on the enterprise side, Uber burned through its entire year's AI budget in three or four months, and another company spent $500 million without knowing where it went — everyone is becoming cost-conscious. The result: only the most important tasks use Anthropic's or OpenAI's models; everything else switches to open-weight models like Kimi K3 — "not everything needs a Cadillac."
  • The line he repeats is: "This industry, despite having already spent several hundred billion dollars, still has no moat." The comparison case is Google Search back in the day — 90% of the world used it without thinking, and that is what a moat looks like; today users rotate between Gemini, ChatGPT and Claude with zero loyalty; developers use Claude, and the moment a better model appears tomorrow they switch immediately, with no lock-in whatsoever.
  • The cloud giants are two-sided: building a data center costs $100 billion, and scale itself is a moat; but their cloud businesses happen to depend on these large-model companies that have no moat — that is their current weakness.
  • His "conspiracy theory": the talk of "AI will destroy the world" is a smokescreen. What the labs really fear is the end of the token-burning era, the absence of a moat, and Chinese open-source models taking share — so they manufacture fear and push for government regulation, because regulation creates a moat: a single ban on using Chinese models and a legal barrier exists instantly, with a duopoly forming along the way. His evidence is simple: if Dario truly believed his own product was so dangerous that it needed to slow down, he should have delayed the IPO — but he didn't. Musk's jab on X (in substance) was: your product will destroy the world? And by the way, how much of the IPO can I get?

4. Where did the cloud giants' cash flow go?

  • Three or four years ago, Microsoft, Amazon and Google made so much money they didn't know what to do with the cash and could only buy back stock; now, because of astronomical data-center capex, the free cash flow is gone — for some, it is even negative.
  • What shocked him most was Google raising $85 billion in equity — a company that has never raised capital since going public and prints cash lying down, issuing new shares: "if you'd told me that a year or two ago, I'd have said you were crazy." The host's figure: the cloud giants' AI capex this year alone is about $700 billion.
  • Michael Burry's depreciation argument (last November the cloud giants stretched chip depreciation schedules from 3–4 years to 5–6 years — flipping a switch to fatten profits — and with so many new chips, old chips become obsolete too fast) gets Eisman's verdict of "too academic": demand is now so large that even old chips are rising in price; if the two labs' growth surges, the depreciation schedule doesn't matter at all; if OpenAI fails and the whole chain reverses, the correction will happen regardless of depreciation — the factors that move the big picture are far bigger than depreciation.

5. Electricity: America's real bottleneck

  • He cites Musk's remark to *The Economist*: China has a chip problem, America has an electricity problem — and China can solve its chip problem, whereas electricity is physical infrastructure that takes a very long time, and no one can be sure how badly it will bind ("get in line; nobody knows").
  • US electricity consumption is growing again for the first time in 15 years, at 3%–4% a year; on the US base, that equals adding the demand of two large cities every year. GE Vernova is his typical beneficiary: when GE was broken up a few years ago, sell-side analysts valued this energy business at negative $3 billion; now its orders are booked out to 2035 and its backlog grew about 85% last quarter. Data centers are powered mainly by gas turbines (turbines are kin to jet engines; one is the size of five houses and takes years to build); when Musk built Colossus in Memphis, the grid couldn't keep up, so he ended up buying 35 mobile gas turbines to generate his own power. Companies like Bloom Energy, which provide small-scale on-site generation, are an alternative.

6. If you must buy AI: buy the ones collecting the cash, not the ones burning it

  • He explicitly won't buy the large-model companies (no moat) and stays wary of the cloud giants (their cash flow is all gone); his approach is to hold "the companies that receive that cash flow": NVIDIA, Micron, GE Vernova, Arista Networks, Cisco, and on the industrial side Eaton, which does electrification — "selling shovels, not flashy software."
  • Within software he distinguishes two fates: the middle layer hollowed out by agents is in danger — he cites Meta's agent product Muse as an example: you tell it to book a flight and a hotel, it runs across every travel site to find the lowest price, and so Booking and Travelocity no longer own the customer; "sidebar ads are useless to AI," and payments could be in the same danger; whereas software deeply embedded in enterprise processes (the kind like Salesforce and ServiceNow that is extremely painful to rip out) is probably fine — what matters is exactly how stable the switching barrier is.
  • Palantir he would love to hold, but at the current price it's too expensive to buy — "it has a story, but be rational."
  • Does it resemble 1999? The architecture is indeed similar (new technology plus financial speculation), but it's too early to conclude: if OpenAI or Anthropic goes down, there will be a real correction, and then the next generation will emerge to clean up the wreckage; whether that happens, he says he doesn't know.

7. His playbook and the Q&A

  • Methodology: look at the story first; he is not a value investor ("cheap stocks are usually cheap for a reason"), but he never carelessly overpays; he likes moats (hence holding Moody's and Visa), though it's not a hard requirement; he hates management selling stock the most; he generally avoids cyclicals (that's just forecasting the economy); he likes companies with a real story and a real growth tailwind. He calls himself a linear thinker, writing in order 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 to the conclusion; his wife Valerie edits his weekly Friday wrap-up and always reverses the order — because most people never read to the end, the conclusion can't be buried at the bottom.
  • US Treasuries (an audience question): the debt is $40 trillion; the average interest rate has risen from 1.77% in 2020 to 3.45%, and annual interest expense from $523 billion to $1.22 trillion. He is skeptical of the debt-spiral theory: the dollar is the global reserve currency and US government debt is the foundation of the global financial system (global banks use it for overnight repo borrowing), so as long as there is no alternative, he is not too worried, though he would prefer a smaller deficit. The number to watch is the 10-year yield, not the federal funds rate: 5% is the market's watershed (he originally thought 4.5% and admits he guessed wrong); on the day of recording it was about 4.98%, having briefly broken above 5% the day before. The real purpose of Bessent's buyback plan is to push down long-end yields and ease the interest burden, but the announced $6 billion is useless against $40 trillion of debt — "so far he has failed miserably" — and he guesses Bessent will try other moves.
  • Precious metals and Bitcoin: he has never bought precious metals; his view is close to Buffett's — it sits there glittering, producing nothing, and has value only because everyone says it does. Bitcoin looks even worse to him: its behavior contradicts its own thesis — by the believers' logic, it should rise on days of inflation worries, rising rates and falling stocks, and fall on days when the Nasdaq surges and rates decline, but in practice it does exactly the opposite; "it seems to be just a speculative asset, a way of speculating on speculation."
  • Geography: in his hedge fund days he invested overseas and found it provided no day-night diversification benefit at all; for years he has invested only in US stocks — "America keeps me busy enough."
  • Asked at the close whether some number would be the trigger (forcing the US to cut benefits or hike rates sharply), his answer returned to politics: the debt problem is purely a political problem, and in Washington today neither party is willing to cut the deficit by even a single dollar.
