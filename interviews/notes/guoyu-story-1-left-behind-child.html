一句话

这是郭宇的童年自述：1991 年生于江西铜矿子弟家庭，父母下海南下后做了四五年留守儿童——单调、孤独、靠读书和写日记打发时间，而正是这段经历埋下了他日后写作、在网上记录自己、以及「不想管人、只想自己做事」的性格底色。

内容要点

出身：三线铜矿子弟 - 出生在江西一个偏僻的铜矿（同江铜矿一类的国营矿山，类似电影《青红》里的三线工厂），去县城要开车两小时。外公外婆从江苏迁来，父亲是江西南部客家人，在子弟小学上学，同学多是矿工子弟和附近农村孩子。 - 矿挖完就破产，如同东北国企下岗潮：职工返原籍或南下。父亲原是电工，他小学二三年级时父亲遭高压电击、从高处坠落进 ICU，虽救回但体力大损、脑血管留下隐患（家族有脑血管基因，父亲后来脑梗、卧床十几年成植物人，与此有关）。

留守的四五年 - 父亲先办「停薪留职」去东莞，自学日语（家里至今留着 NHK 寄来的广播日语教材），转做机床维修与销售，赶上南方小厂进口德日机床的窗口赚到了钱，在深圳买房；母亲随后也南下。郭宇由外公外婆带大，从小学到初一下学期末（快要到初二）才被接到深圳。 - 童年关键词是「单调」：没有商店书店，骑车几小时去县城书店看一下午；小姨、舅舅在上海工作、1998 年小姨公派留学去美国前常寄书和巧克力，格林童话借给同学「有借无回」翻烂为止。 - 1998 年（7 岁）第一次去上海送小姨出国：小姨答应带他吃必胜客却没时间，他自己带外婆坐地铁找店、坐过站到外滩，最后吃了麦当劳，一个外国大爷送了他开心乐园餐的蓝色小玩具——记到今天。 - 暑假最无聊：同学要么出去玩、要么帮农活带弟妹，他不睡午觉，一个人在矿山里转、自己跟自己打篮球；寄住上海亲戚家体验也不好（被城里小孩看不起、住房挤、矛盾多），宁愿自己在乡下待着。冬天帮父亲看铁皮板车小摊，手冻伤留疤、不敢跟父母说。

三件影响一生的事 1. 读书与作家梦：第一本长篇是 1998 年在上海看的《海底两万里》，之后看「书虫」双语缩写本、萌芽系的韩寒郭敬明——小镇青年靠写作改变命运的路径让他幻想自己也能靠写作走出去，作文常拿范文。 2. 写日记：因为「没有人可以说话」，从小每天写日记，写满很多本子；有网络后转到网上发（最早饭否，高中时），延续成今天在 X 上把生活和想法当记录写下来的习惯。 3. 游戏与编程的入口：父母离开后成绩下滑、沉迷网吧（先局域网、后网游），在深圳黑网吧还遇过警察、从后门跑。学校暑期机房兴趣班本是冲着玩电脑去的，却学了 QBasic、Pascal——这是编程的种子；真正下决心学代码是高三毕业后：父亲病重、母亲没学历没文凭、什么活都干（做家政时带他一起，手把手教他打扫卫生的讲究，后来靠认真被推荐去龙岗区政府后勤管几十人，却说管人太麻烦——「不想管人」这一点他说像母亲，也像他自己），他必须尽快经济独立，大学起就靠写代码自己挣钱。

性格注脚 - 小学三四年级脾气火爆：同桌在墙上画画、班主任冤枉是他画的，他掀了课桌在教室里和老师打起来；成绩好但混的是乡下小混混圈子。他说这种直脾气在字节「对事不对人」的环境里倒常见——程序员在会议室跟老板大嗓门吵架是常态；如今生活简单、不依赖别人，冲突才少了。

In One Sentence

This is Guo Yu's account of his childhood: born in 1991 into a copper-mine workers' family in Jiangxi, he spent four or five years as a left-behind child after his parents went south to Hainan — a monotonous, lonely time passed by reading and keeping a diary; and it was precisely this experience that planted the seeds of his later writing, of recording his life online, and of his disposition of "not wanting to manage people, only wanting to do things on his own".

Key Points

Background: a child of a Third Front copper mine - He was born at a remote copper mine in Jiangxi (a state-run mine like the Tongjiang Copper Mine, similar to the Third Front factories in the film Shanghai Dreams), a two-hour drive from the county town. His maternal grandparents had migrated from Jiangsu; his father was a Hakka from southern Jiangxi. He attended the mine's school for employees' children, and his classmates were mostly miners' children and kids from nearby villages. - When the ore ran out, the mine went bankrupt, much like the wave of layoffs at state-owned enterprises in Northeast China: employees returned to their hometowns or headed south. His father had been an electrician; when Guo Yu was in second or third grade of primary school, his father suffered a high-voltage electric shock, fell from a height, and ended up in the ICU. He survived, but his physical strength was greatly diminished and his cerebral blood vessels were left with hidden damage (the family has a genetic predisposition to cerebrovascular problems; his father later had a cerebral infarction and spent over a decade bedridden in a vegetative state, which was related to this).

Four or five years left behind - His father first took "unpaid leave while keeping his post" and went to Dongguan, taught himself Japanese (the family still keeps the radio Japanese textbooks NHK mailed to him), and switched to machine-tool repair and sales, making money during the window when small factories in the south were importing German and Japanese machine tools; he bought a home in Shenzhen. His mother then also went south. Guo Yu was raised by his maternal grandparents, and only in the second semester of his first year of junior high (nearly his second year) was he brought to Shenzhen. - The keyword of his childhood was "monotony": there were no shops or bookstores, and he would cycle for hours to a bookstore in the county town to read for an afternoon. His aunt and uncle worked in Shanghai, and before his aunt was sent to study in the US on a government program in 1998, she often mailed him books and chocolates; he lent his copy of Grimm's Fairy Tales to classmates and it was passed around until it fell apart, never returned. - In 1998 (aged 7), he went to Shanghai for the first time to see his aunt off abroad: his aunt had promised to take him to Pizza Hut but had no time, so he took his grandmother on the subway by himself to find the restaurant, rode past their stop to the Bund, and ended up eating at McDonald's, where a foreign man gave him the little blue toy from his Happy Meal — he remembers it to this day. - Summer holidays were the most boring: classmates were either out playing or helping with farm work and looking after younger siblings, while he didn't nap and wandered around the mine alone, playing basketball by himself. Staying with relatives in Shanghai was not a good experience either (looked down on by city kids, cramped housing, lots of friction), so he preferred staying in the countryside on his own. In winter he helped his father watch a small tin-cart stall; his hands got frostbite that left scars, and he didn't dare tell his parents.

Three things that shaped his life 1. Reading and the dream of becoming a writer: The first full-length novel he read was Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas, read in Shanghai in 1998; afterwards he read the abridged bilingual "Bookworm" editions and the Mengya-circle writers Han Han and Guo Jingming — the path of small-town youths changing their fate through writing made him fantasize that he too could write his way out, and his school essays were often used as model compositions. 2. Keeping a diary: Because there was "no one to talk to", he wrote in a diary every day from a young age, filling many notebooks; once he had internet access he moved it online (first on Fanfou, in high school), which developed into his present-day habit of recording his life and thoughts on X. 3. The gateway to games and programming: After his parents left, his grades slipped and he became addicted to internet cafés (first LAN games, then online games); at a black internet café in Shenzhen he once ran into the police and escaped through the back door. He joined the school's summer computer-room interest class just to play on computers, but ended up learning QBasic and Pascal — the seed of his programming. He truly resolved to learn coding after graduating from high school: his father was seriously ill, his mother had no education or credentials and did whatever work she could find (when doing domestic work she took him along and taught him the finer points of cleaning by hand; later, because of her conscientiousness, she was recommended to manage dozens of people in the logistics department of the Longgang District government, but said managing people was too much trouble — he says this "not wanting to manage people" trait of his is like his mother's, and like himself too). He had to become financially independent as soon as possible, and from university onward he earned his own money by writing code.

A note on his temperament - In third and fourth grade of primary school he had a fiery temper: his deskmate drew on the wall, the head teacher wrongly blamed him for it, and he flipped over his desk and got into a fight with the teacher in the classroom; he had good grades but ran with a crowd of small-time rural delinquents. He says this kind of direct temper is actually common in ByteDance's "focus on the matter, not the person" environment — programmers shouting at their bosses in meeting rooms is routine; nowadays his life is simple and he doesn't depend on others, so there are fewer conflicts.
