纪要｜《第5集｜郭宇谈财富、投资与退休》

视频：https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tWmNN87VvcE ｜时长 79 分钟｜播客《一代马之外》第 5 集，主持人 Randy、GeekPlux ⚠️ 时效提示：本集录制于约 2023 年（谈 NFT、其日本公司 Checks 运营中、疫情后初期），属郭宇系列里最「考古」的一集，观点请按当时的语境读。 说明：机器转写，个别专名有误听（多说转写为「多數」，沈正宇转写为「鎮宇」，豌豆荚转写为「弯斗甲」等），已按上下文校正；后半段为采样通读。金额与收益均为其自述口径。

一句话

这是理解郭宇其余所有视频的「前传」：一个政治学出身、父亲久病卧床的普通程序员，如何靠 2013 年一笔无心插柳的港股定投 + 一次「主动选择不稳定人生」的跳槽，在 28 岁攒够离开的底气。他对财富自由的定义冷静得近乎扫兴：它是一道数学题——先量清楚你一年到底花多少钱，剩下的只是 10 倍与 4% 的事。 而财富带来的最大苦恼不是没钱花，是「你能去任何地方」的不确定性本身（本集标题即出于此）。

职业线：每一步都是「离开舒适区」

  • 2008 年高考后第三天开始自学代码（家里条件不好、父亲生病卧床十几年、后来成植物人，他必须能自己挣钱养家）。大二腾讯实习被刷，大三进支付宝体验技术部（2011–2013），住同学宿舍苦练技术——那个年代一线工程师多非科班出身，因为顶尖学生都去投行和外企，互联网公司是「成绩没那么好的人」去的，机会反而公平。
  • 支付宝时期生活极其安逸：六点下班、西湖边喝茶；同批同事两年内买房（杭州房价六千多一平）结婚生子。他觉得「一眼望到头」，2013 年辞职——父亲当时还有意识，非常生气。暑假去北京看了豌豆荚等公司，被中关村的氛围击中：他承认自己内心追逐的就是不稳定的生活——只有承担风险才有更大收益，这是他和同批人最大的分野。
  • 加入沈正宇的多说（兼维护图虫），2014 年 12 月被字节收购。字节六年半是大小周、凌晨两三点下班的日子（《百万英雄》两周三班倒赶出来）。2015 年头条瓶颈期很多人走，他留下来的理由很特别：这家公司是 AI 驱动的——推荐工程师坦承「我们不知道引擎怎么工作，只能做实验调参数」，他觉得这种「工程师不知道自己代码如何运行」的范式酷到不行、必将改变所有互联网产品。今天生成式 AI 时代人人如此，当年只有字节在这么干。

「酒店玩家」的真相：累出来的预算学

外人看他朋友圈天天飞、住酒店、泡日本温泉，以为他没在上班。真相：工作太累、休息太少，不安排就会抑郁。他从 2013 年起研究常旅客体系（给一家常旅 App 做过技术顾问），每年做旅行预算、双十一把下一年的机票全买完——假期固定、提前锁定，再用各家 loyalty program 之间汇率不一致的套利把成本压到最低，让两天假也能彻底切换到另一种生活方式（reset）。状态管理不好，就做不了理性决策——找工作如此，投资也如此。 这是全集最被低估的一句。

退休决策：想了三年

  • 2017 年动念：抖音 2016 年 12 月发布后，他判断视频推荐是唯一能无文化障碍全球化的模式（此前头条在巴西、日本推图文产品都因语言推荐引擎太重而失败），自己的股票「有一个非常确定的大 potential」。随后进入漫长的纠结期：去哪个国家、要不要在北京置业——其间父亲、奶奶相继去世，不确定性最折磨人的地方在于「没有家庭束缚的人，能去任何地方，所以必须非常谨慎地选」。
  • 2019 年提离职、交接半年，2020 年 2 月在日本远程离职（疫情）。那篇退休信 2 月发时无人问津，6 月被知乎一个问题带火。后来的事（字节不上市、中美交恶、阿里一度跌到 2000 多亿美元）全不在他 2019 年的剧本里，但因为生活方案已经定死，对他个人「没什么太大影响」——计划的价值不是预测对，而是让你对波动免疫。

财富来源：纠正一个流行误会

外界普遍以为他靠字节股权自由。他本人拆账：字节工资很低、股权至今大部分没卖。真正的积累来自投资——而且起点荒诞：2013 年在深圳高新园地铁站，天天有人塞港股开户传单，他被烦到「开了个户」了事，用招商证券每月把存下的钱扔进去定投腾讯，没有任何策略，一投五年（腾讯从 30 多港元涨到 500 多）；另有 2013 年买入的比特币。2018–19 年因为要靠投资生活才开始认真研究：300 多开始卖腾讯、最高 500 多出清，换仓美股，正好赶上疫情前完成转移、又赶上 2021 年美股大涨——他反复强调这是运气。他的自由线：大几百万到小一千万本金、年化 5–6%、一个人一年花几十万，绰绰有余；实际结果已远超。日本的几处房产是 2018–19 年买的，在组合里占比不大，只图每月日元租金现金流覆盖当地生活费，不必从美股取钱换汇。他每年只花「赚到的利息」，但承认花钱会随赚钱水涨船高。

给程序员的理财建议（本集最实用的部分）

  1. 先看清自己的风险结构：多数工程师的收入靠薪资（大头）、财富靠房产（大头）——两个集中度叠在一起极其危险。35 岁被裁员，可支配收入瞬间少一半甚至八成，再背着房贷、房子又卖不掉，就是流动性危机。日本发生过的事，可能在任何国家重演。
  2. 股市里 90% 的人亏钱：长桥排行榜上年赚 15% 就能进前 10%。所以要像学写代码一样学投资：看文档、看视频、看前人经验，用小钱真实下场找到自己的交易风格——「不然你永远不知道自己是什么样的交易者」。
  3. 不想当 trader，就直接买指数，像大部分美国人一样，别折腾。券商希望你频繁交易（手续费、融资费），会不断给你推期权期货这类你根本不懂风险的东西——DeFi 和 CEX 的用户教育之差也在这里。
  4. 预测市场几乎不可能：美国市场太深（特斯拉一天几百亿美元成交量），中国工程师不擅长玩西方人的游戏、又没经验，老实承认这一点就是进步。
  5. 财务自由的操作定义：认真量自己过去两年的年支出；如果对现在的生活已经满意，年支出 ×10 左右的金融资产（哪怕买美债、按 4% 提取）就够了。降支出靠地理套利：泰国、巴厘岛、日本乡下（冲浪的人一周上两天班打零工、不婚不育）；在乡下你想花都花不出去多少。他给中国的「中产焦虑」降了温：在小红书语境外，有 5000 万纯金融资产（不含房子）绝对算富人。

退休后的新事业：卖数据

录制时他在日本经营公司 Checks（与日本合伙人荒川等），招了不少国内朋友过去。做的事：把会员卡、消费小票、信用卡的消费数据（在日本法律框架与用户授权下）电子化，卖给需要消费数据的企业——他的判断是，NFT 定义了数字产权之后，「个人产生的数据值多少钱」会成为一个巨大的消费市场（日本已有拍小票换钱的产品，一天限五张；消费数据甚至能预测大宗商品走势）。他 2011 年在支付宝做年度账单时就意识到平台「比你自己更先知道你将要发生什么事」，只是大公司没把价值还给个人。选日本不选新加坡/美国的理由：新加坡太小、生活单调且对外国人区别对待；日本法律上对外国人和本国人一视同仁、会日语就基本等于本地人，加上生活品质与温泉。对想移居的人他的建议是：别仓促，先以居民身份来住一段，找一个和你处境相同（有孩/无孩差别巨大）的当地人一对一聊，博客和视频都有幸存者偏差。

This is the "prequel" to understanding all of Guo Yu's other videos: how an ordinary programmer — a political science graduate whose father was bedridden for years — built the confidence to leave at 28, through an unintentional 2013 Hong Kong stock regular investment in Tencent plus one deliberate leap to "choose an unstable life." His definition of financial freedom is calm to the point of being a letdown: it is a math problem — first measure clearly how much you actually spend in a year, and the rest is just a matter of 10x and 4%. And the greatest trouble wealth brings is not having no money to spend, but the uncertainty of "you can go anywhere" itself (the episode's title comes from this).

Career Line: Every Step Was "Leaving the Comfort Zone"

  • He began teaching himself to code on the third day after the 2008 college entrance exam (his family was not well off, his father was bedridden for over a decade and later became vegetative, so he had to be able to earn money to support the family). He was rejected from a Tencent internship in his sophomore year, joined Alipay's Experience Technology Department in his junior year (2011–2013), and lived in a classmate's dorm grinding his skills — in that era most frontline engineers were not computer-science graduates, because top students all went to investment banks and foreign companies, and internet companies were where "students with not-so-good grades" went, so opportunities were actually fair.
  • Life at Alipay was extremely comfortable: off work at six, drinking tea by West Lake; peers from the same cohort bought homes within two years (Hangzhou housing was over 6,000 yuan per square meter) and got married and had children. He felt he could "see to the end at a glance," and quit in 2013 — his father was still conscious at the time and was very angry. During the summer break he went to Beijing and visited companies like Wandoujia, and was struck by the atmosphere in Zhongguancun: he admits what he was chasing inside was precisely an unstable life — only by taking risks could there be greater returns, and this was his biggest divergence from his cohort.
  • He joined Shen Zhengyu's Duoshuo (while also maintaining Tuchong), which was acquired by ByteDance in December 2014. His six and a half years at ByteDance were days of big-week/small-week schedules and getting off work at two or three in the morning (Million Heroes was rushed out in two weeks on three shifts). During Toutiao's bottleneck period in 2015 many people left; his reason for staying was unusual: this company was AI-driven — recommendation engineers frankly admitted "we don't know how the engine works, we can only run experiments and tune parameters," and he thought this paradigm of "engineers not knowing how their own code runs" was incredibly cool and would inevitably change all internet products. Today in the generative AI era everyone is like this; back then only ByteDance worked that way.

The Truth About the "Hotel Player": Budgeting Learned from Exhaustion

Outsiders saw his Moments full of flights, hotels, and Japanese hot springs and assumed he wasn't working. The truth: work was too exhausting and rest too scarce, and without planning he would become depressed. From 2013 he studied frequent-traveler systems (he was a technical advisor for a frequent-travel app), made a travel budget every year, and bought all of the next year's flights during Double Eleven — holidays fixed and locked in early — then used arbitrage from inconsistent exchange rates between different loyalty programs to push costs to the minimum, so that even a two-day break could fully switch him to another way of life (a reset). If you don't manage your state well, you can't make rational decisions — true for job hunting, and true for investing. This is the most underrated line in the whole episode.

The Retirement Decision: Three Years of Thinking

  • The idea came in 2017: after Douyin launched in December 2016, he judged that video recommendation was the only model that could globalize without cultural barriers (before that, Toutiao's image-and-text products in Brazil and Japan had both failed because the language recommendation engine was too heavy), and his own stock had "a very certain, large potential." Then came a long period of agonizing: which country to go to, whether to buy property in Beijing — during which his father and grandmother passed away in succession. The most tormenting thing about uncertainty was that "a person with no family ties can go anywhere, so must choose very carefully."
  • He submitted his resignation in 2019, handed over for half a year, and left remotely from Japan in February 2020 (the pandemic). His retirement letter drew no attention when posted in February, then went viral in June thanks to a Zhihu question. What happened later (ByteDance not going public, US-China tensions, Alibaba once falling to just over 200 billion US dollars) was all outside his 2019 script, but because his life plan was already fixed, it had "not much real impact" on him personally — the value of a plan is not predicting correctly, but making you immune to volatility.

Source of Wealth: Correcting a Popular Misconception

It is widely assumed he achieved freedom through ByteDance equity. He breaks down the accounts himself: ByteDance pay was very low, and most of the equity still hasn't been sold to this day. The real accumulation came from investing — and the starting point was absurd: in 2013 at the Shenzhen High-Tech Park metro station, people stuffed Hong Kong stock account-opening flyers at him every day, until he was annoyed enough to "just open an account," using China Merchants Securities to throw his monthly savings into a regular investment in Tencent, with no strategy at all, for five years (Tencent rose from over 30 Hong Kong dollars to over 500); he also bought Bitcoin in 2013. Only in 2018–19, because he needed to live off investing, did he start studying seriously: he began selling Tencent from the 300s, cleared out at a high of over 500, rotated into US stocks, happening to complete the transfer just before the pandemic and catching the big 2021 US stock rally — he repeatedly stresses this was luck. His freedom line: a principal of several million to just under ten million, annualized 5–6%, one person spending a few hundred thousand a year, is more than enough; the actual result has far exceeded that. The several properties in Japan were bought in 2018–19 and are a small share of the portfolio, only for the monthly yen rental cash flow to cover local living costs, so he doesn't need to draw money from US stocks and convert currency. He only spends the "interest earned" each year, but admits spending rises as earnings rise.

Financial Advice for Programmers (The Most Practical Part of This Episode)

  1. First see your own risk structure clearly: Most engineers' income relies on salary (the bulk) and wealth on property (the bulk) — two concentrations stacked together are extremely dangerous. Laid off at 35, disposable income instantly drops by half or even 80%, while carrying a mortgage on a house that can't be sold — that is a liquidity crisis. What happened in Japan could repeat in any country.
  2. 90% of people lose money in the stock market: On Longbridge leaderboards, earning 15% in a year puts you in the top 10%. So learn investing the way you learn to code: read documentation, watch videos, read predecessors' experience, and use small amounts of real money to find your own trading style — "otherwise you'll never know what kind of trader you are."
  3. If you don't want to be a trader, just buy the index, like most Americans, and don't tinker. Brokers want you to trade frequently (commissions, margin fees) and will keep pushing you things like options and futures whose risks you don't understand at all — the difference in user education between DeFi and CEXes is also here.
  4. Predicting the market is almost impossible: The US market is too deep (Tesla trades tens of billions of US dollars a day); Chinese engineers are not good at playing Westerners' games and have no experience, and honestly admitting this is progress.
  5. An operational definition of financial freedom: Carefully measure your annual spending over the past two years; if you are already satisfied with your current life, financial assets of about 10x your annual spending (even buying US Treasuries and withdrawing at 4%) are enough. Cut spending through geographic arbitrage: Thailand, Bali, rural Japan (surfers work odd jobs two days a week, unmarried and childless); in the countryside you couldn't spend much even if you wanted to. He cooled down China's "middle-class anxiety": outside the Xiaohongshu context, having 50 million yuan in pure financial assets (excluding a house) definitely counts as rich.

A New Venture After Retirement: Selling Data

At the time of recording he was running the company Checks in Japan (with Japanese partners including Arakawa), and had recruited quite a few friends from China to go over. What they do: digitize consumption data from membership cards, receipts, and credit cards (within Japan's legal framework and with user authorization) and sell it to companies that need consumer data — his judgment is that after NFTs defined digital property rights, "how much is the data an individual generates worth" will become a huge consumer market (Japan already has products that pay you for photographing receipts, limited to five a day; consumer data can even predict commodity price trends). Back in 2011, working on annual bills at Alipay, he realized platforms "know what is about to happen to you before you do yourself"; big companies just never returned the value to individuals. His reasons for choosing Japan over Singapore / the US: Singapore is too small, life is monotonous, and it treats foreigners differently; Japan treats foreigners and nationals equally under the law, speaking Japanese basically makes you a local, plus the quality of life and hot springs. His advice for people who want to emigrate: don't rush; first come and live for a while as a resident, find a local in the same situation as you (having children vs. not makes a huge difference) for a one-on-one chat; blogs and videos all have survivorship bias.
