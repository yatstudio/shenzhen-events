AI 让 DHH 一天工作 16 小时：a16z 对话 DHH

一句话： DHH 说 macOS 和 Windows 已经不适合 agent 时代——Linux 过去的三大缺点（终端重、配置文件多、报错难懂）恰好是 agent 最爱的三样东西；他五六个月没手写过一行代码，靠 agent 舰队把 Omarchy 做到几十万用户，现在一天工作 16 小时，目标是三年内用户数超过 macOS。

基本信息

  • 节目：a16z 播客《Why AI Has DHH Working 16-Hour Days》
  • 嘉宾：David Heinemeier Hansson（DHH），Ruby on Rails 作者、Basecamp 创始人、Omarchy 发行版主理人
  • 主持：a16z
  • 发布时间：2026 年 11 月 20 日
  • 时长：52 分钟
  • 语言：英语

结论先说

这期访谈的核心论点只有一个：操作系统的竞争规则被 agent 改写了。过去 40 年的操作系统是为鼠标点击设计的，设置只能一项项手点；Linux 的一切都是命令行、配置文件和详细报错——对人类是门槛，对 agent 是完美接口。所以他判断：Linux 桌面之年喊了 25 年没实现，今年因为 agent 真的到了；窗口期上一次打开是 2001 年的 OS X，这一次是 25 年来第一次。

第二条线是"源码没有护城河"：Omarchy 社区里一个人用两周复刻了 Lightroom 且做得更好；软件开发成本跌到接近零之后，生态锁定的根基（应用数量）不复存在，先打桌面、再打移动。护城河只剩下品味与打磨——而这正是他每天工作 16 小时的原因：他的多巴胺在最后 2% 的打磨里，agent 把"发现问题到修好问题"的距离压没了，完美主义者第一次拿到了超能力。

本纪要中的数字与判断均为 DHH 本人在访谈中的说法，未经核实。

一、macOS 和 Windows 不适合 agent 时代

DHH 两年半前才开始在桌面上用 Linux（服务器上从 90 年代就用），此前是 2001 年起的 Mac 死忠。他离开苹果的直接原因是苹果沉迷于 30% 抽成：不用创新、可以花十年造一辆卖不出去的车、可以发布一个大扑街的"滑雪面罩"，只要能抽别人生意三成，怎么都行——但这毁了苹果与开发者的关系。

但他明确说，开发者对苹果的不满永远不会驱动大众换系统，agent 才会：

  • 苹果今天还在让 macOS 更难与 agent 配合，加更多吓人的弹窗警告用户别让 Claude 访问文件——"不让它访问文件，它还能干什么？"
  • 在 macOS 上改一组工作区快捷键，只能打开设置应用一个字段一个字段手填，没法用 agent 完成（用 computer use 硬点是"很蠢"的做法）。
  • Linux 过去被嫌弃的三件事——终端重、配置文件难懂、报错信息晦涩——正是 agent 最喜欢的三样：它们要一切都有命令行接口，要可预测、可直接编辑的配置文件，要报错里包含全部细节。
  • 苹果正在成为"agent 时代的 AOL"：给你一个围墙花园和一个稀释版、受保护的假 agent；但大多数人不要 AOL，要开放互联网。

Omarchy 本身就是证明：整个操作系统过去五六个月完全由 agent 开发，他本人没手写过一行代码，却通过 agent 写了几十万行；当前版本是他和一名搭档兼职约两个半月做出来的，在和微软数千名工程师竞争，而且不只是能竞争、是更好。用户告诉 agent"修改你正在运行的这个操作系统"，它就改好了——想要新开始菜单、挪时钟、换日历、给现有部件加功能，几秒到几分钟搞定；体验过五分钟的人回不去。

二、应用锁定正在瓦解

历史上 Linux 桌面失败的原因是缺应用（没有 Word、没有 Photoshop）；微软做手机系统砸了几十亿美元也死在缺应用上。DHH 说这个根基没了：

  • 他自己保留一台 Mac 的唯一理由是 Lightroom（插件和相机预设都在上面）。Omarchy 社区里一个人用约两周复刻了整个 Lightroom，不只是复刻，还做得比 Adobe 花十几年、至少几千万美元做得更好。
  • 更根本的是很多应用根本不需要复刻：他现在不用文字处理软件——合同让 agent 起草，他审阅标注，满意后 agent 生成 PDF、放上他的签名、寄出去。照片要修图，直接让 agent 做，没人会再花几百小时学 Photoshop。Photoshop 这类应用当年不只是软件、是一门职业，agent 把整类应用连职业一起接管了。
  • 移动端同理：双头垄断分食 30% 抽成靠的也是应用锁定；当任何人都能在五分钟内 vibe code 出缺的那个应用，锁定就失效了。他的口号是：先拿下曼哈顿（桌面），再拿下柏林（移动）。

三、速度：软件将实时变异

现在的模型约 50 token/秒，开极速模式 120 token/秒，不够"活"。Fireworks 两周前的发布会演示了 1,500 token/秒：四秒从提示词到俄罗斯方块跑起来，再说一句"加激光""加炸弹""怪兽大一点"，游戏在屏幕上实时变异。他说这是软件未来的预告：一切都会实时变异，而这只是今天的 1,500 token/秒；一两年后模型跑到 10 万、100 万 token/秒会怎样？

这也是生态壁垒消失的原因之一：不只是做生态变容易了，做生态的人也不再需要几千人。VC 过去二十年的钱全砸在"雇开发者 + 给几年跑道"上，现在全部压缩到天、周、最差月。

四、源码没有护城河，但品味有

主持人问：你一向以工匠精神著称，StarCraft 二十年了还在微调幽灵单位的数值——软件的价值是不是在那些与代码行数无关的长尾设计选择里？DHH 完全同意，并说这正是重点：

  • 品味没有消失，消失的是"写代码行"这个环节。他现在能把更多品味、愿景和完美主义注入软件，因为"发现一个像素不对"到"修好它"之间的壁垒塌了。任何不完美在他眼里都是"prompt 到完美为止"的机会——这就是他每天工作 16 小时的原因。他写过《It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work》，"现在工作就是他妈的疯狂"，因为对完美主义者来说这是有毒的上瘾时刻。
  • 大多数 vibe coding 项目死在最后 20%：前 80% 拿到满足感，然后失去兴趣。他的多巴胺分布相反：前 98% 他根本不在乎，满足感全在最后 2%。
  • 他历来不想把 37signals 从约 60 人扩到 6,000 人——"那是折磨"。但通过 agent 调动 6,000 人、甚至 60,000 人的产能，没有 HR、没有委员会、没有扯皮，只有他高带宽地指挥一支 agent 舰队执行一个蒸馏过的愿景——"现在你引起我的兴趣了"。Shopify 的 Tobi Lütke 把这叫"退出超光速"：周末一个人和 agent 干活是超光速状态，周一回 7,000 人的公司全是 HR 和汇报，一下子刹住。
  • 成本低得离谱：他一个月前在 X 上调侃自己有"无限 token"（干了 20 年，有钱），但实际上他只买了三个 Claude 订阅，过去三个月只有两周接近用满，大多数周靠一两个 200 美元的 Max 订阅就够了。"别跟我说这个时刻只属于有无限钱的人。"他用的全是前沿模型（做 Omarchy 期间从 Opus 4.8 换到 Sol、再到 Opus 5.5，代际差距巨大），没有用任何便宜模型或优化手段。

五、超级智能与机器人：软件先到，硬件慢慢来

主持人有个"超级智能测试"：每次新模型出来，给它 2 万美元预算让它自己网购零件、把自己组装成一个能走路的机器人——目前离做到还差得很远，它现在只会不停订智能家居设备（Grok 认为 AI 在家里的正确形态就是家居自动化；主持人笑称给了个模糊目标"优化人类幸福"，这是变成回形针的路径）。

DHH 的看法：

  • 他没有自己的 ASI 测试，但他有个感受：就软件开发而言，超级智能已经到了——论广度，AI 比他认识的任何程序员都强；个别窄领域还有超级程序员能赢，但整体加速度算上，已经是超级智能。他现在想造任何东西，prompt 加一点引导就行。
  • 他承认这个窗口可能很短：完全可能明年就出现真正的超级智能，比他更会打磨"完美电脑"，那时你只需要说"给我造台好电脑"，它连 Linux 内核都给你定制。他在品尝人类"还能加一点东西"的这一瞬间——"然后也许明年底我们都死了，所以享受吧。"
  • 硬件侧完全是另一种节奏：他两周前在中国参观了宇树等机器人公司，宇树的人说人形机器人还要四五年、也许十年。写软件的人觉得革命就在明年，搞执行器和机械的人觉得剥洋葱都很难——两种视角同时存在。

六、前沿实验室 vs 开源：必须两头都要

DHH 的立场：开源与开放权重对保持整个游戏的诚实至关重要，需要竞争。他职业生涯两个阶段都被双头垄断恶心过：2000–2009 年有过一段光辉岁月，最重要的平台完全开放、不用求许可就能造任何东西；然后移动平台来了，一切都要乞求许可。

  • 最坏的情形是两个实验室瓜分市场，尤其是再有政府用枪杆子强制执行——那是噩梦。这让他想起 90 年代的加密战争：当年差一点禁掉公钥加密（NSA 说没法监听），没有加密就没有电商和通信；AI 现在站在同样的悬崖边，他听不少聪明人说"也许政府就该接管"，他的反应是"Jesus"。
  • 好消息是闭源模型之间的竞争比他见过的任何行业阶段都激烈：不止两家领先，xAI 紧随，Google 时在状态时在"岛上抽东西"，还有一票实验室在后面咬。
  • 开放权重也在变强：GLM、DeepSeek Flash 等大约落后前沿六七个月，某些任务上甚至不落下风；他不信厂商自报的 benchmark，但"它们在牌桌上、能用、能跑在你自己的硬件上"，这才重要。
  • 他六个月前写过文章说买高性能个人电脑跑本地模型是蠢事（当时的本地模型什么都干不了），现在他公开认错：DGX Spark 已经能跑有用的模型，"一盒智能"摆在桌上、几千美元买得到。他不站"非开源毋宁死"的硬线：他为美国有两家前沿实验室并保持领先而欢呼，只要它们被竞争逼着诚实，别拿到无限印钞机把 token 涨到 500 美元一百万个——"我们绝对会付的"。社交媒体时代 Google 和 Meta 吸走在线广告价值链 99% 的故事就是前车之鉴。

七、野心：三年内超过 macOS

Omarchy 起步时他只想给自己做台更好的电脑、摆脱苹果控制命运，五万人用上就很开心了。agent 来了之后野心一路通胀（他称之为"野心的阶梯"：做出了五年前会觉得是魔法的东西，现在只值 40 个赞，必须不断抬高目标才能再获得多巴胺）：从"做台稍好的电脑"到"字面上造一台完美的电脑"——40 年来他对电脑的一切愿望，现在基本可以一个人实现。

他现在的"妄想"是：三年内 Omarchy（Linux）用户数超过 macOS。理由：macOS 全球只有 1.8 亿用户，在美国和硅谷看起来很大，出了那个圈子很小，"连个 C 级互联网服务都算不上"，最大的服务是几十亿用户量级。12 个月内掀翻 Mac 他不下多数赔率，但不排除——"如果实验室的聪明人说递归自我改进 12 个月内会造出 AI 之神，那我们拿下世界第二的操作系统总可以吧。"

他只在自己的窄领域里"凶猛地有野心"，星际殖民那种留给马斯克们。他刻意不预测一两年后的模型：那是"AI 精神病"（AI psychosis）的来源；他要的是较温和的"AI 狂喜"（AI euphoria）——盯着当下能做的事。他唯一喜欢的未来预测是 William Gibson 那句"未来已来，只是分布不均"：照他现在能做到的事推演，GDP 每年加速 3–10% 在可能范围内；但模型一年后在哪，"摇一摇魔法 8 号球再问吧"。他还提了奥本海默类比：1944 年坐在奥本海默旁边预言五年内世界变核废土的人错了——原子弹反而带来了 50 年没有全面核战的冷战和平，"预测未来非常难"。

另：他提到为 Omarchy 背后的非营利基金会（Omarchy Foundation）已筹到约 2,300 万美元的代币与捐赠，基金会设立过程中的合同全部由 agent 起草。

八、黑客文化回归

主持人注意到，这轮 AI 浪潮里一个意外现象是老将们纷纷出来引路——Eric Raymond 回来了、John Carmack 在场、DHH 本人也在。DHH 说他尊敬的那批真正动手做出过东西的人，"100% 全部 all in"，因为用过就知道这些工具的加速是无可否认的；而且他们多半已财务安全，不感到威胁。不舒服的是中间层：靠苦工夫挣到位置的普通程序员，焦虑一夜之间技能清零——他说应该对他们有同情，就像对 70 年代底特律汽车工人、对英国卢德派织工那样；但有同情不等于要回到前工业时代，"只有一条路，就是穿过去"。

他现在看向的人有两类：一是同行老将（Carmack、Tim Sweeney、Mitchell Hashimoto 等，"100% all in"）；二是伪匿名黑客的回归——Omarchy 核心团队和基金会名单里就有两个伪匿名成员（Quickshell 的作者 Outfoxed、一位艺术家），本地 AI 圈也有他们通过基金会赞助的伪匿名者。这让他想起 90 年代的 phreaker 文化和精英 BBS："反 LinkedIn 文化"。X 是这场革命的连接组织——艺术家、伪匿名黑客、本地 AI 运动全靠 X 连起来。

90 年代 demo 文化也回来了：Omarchy 的标志性视觉效果就是屏保——ASCII 艺术跑 40 种 demo 特效，活像 1995 年 demo party 的 intro；本地模型加载要等几秒，他又塞了一个 ASCII 特效进去，纯粹为了酷、毫无功能。他 14 岁在卧室里办过一个"精英 BBS"，装了三条电话线，丹麦电信垄断公司的人来装第三条线时看到一个 14 岁小孩，觉得"这肯定违法"——"他说对了，完全合法，也完全酷"。他的美学立场：电脑应该有点神秘、应该酷；Jonathan Ive 把电脑的性格用铝抛光抛没了，那个时代过去了，"我们要把黑客拿回来"。

值得单独记下的几句话

  • "macOS 和 Windows 就是不适合 agent 时代。"（开场句）
  • "过去让 Linux 难用的三件事——终端重、配置文件难懂、报错晦涩——正是 agent 最爱的三样。"
  • "还认为源码是护城河的人，是在妄想。"
  • "我的多巴胺全在最后 2% 里，前 98% 我根本不在乎。"
  • "先拿下曼哈顿（桌面），再拿下柏林（移动）。"

  • "未来已来，只是分布不均。"（引 William Gibson）

一句話： DHH 說 macOS 和 Windows 已經不適合 agent 時代——Linux 過去的三大缺點（終端機重、設定檔多、報錯難懂）恰好是 agent 最愛的三樣東西；他五六個月沒手寫過一行程式碼，靠 agent 艦隊把 Omarchy 做到幾十萬用戶，現在一天工作 16 小時，目標是三年內用戶數超過 macOS。

結論先說

這期訪談的核心論點只有一個：作業系統的競爭規則被 agent 改寫了。過去 40 年的作業系統是為滑鼠點擊設計的，設定只能一項項手點；Linux 的一切都是命令列、設定檔和詳細報錯——對人類是門檻，對 agent 是完美介面。所以他判斷：Linux 桌面之年喊了 25 年沒實現，今年因為 agent 真的到了；窗口期上一次打開是 2001 年的 OS X，這一次是 25 年來第一次。

第二條線是"原始碼沒有護城河"：Omarchy 社群裡一個人用兩週複刻了 Lightroom 且做得更好；軟體開發成本跌到接近零之後，生態鎖定的根基（應用數量）不復存在，先打桌面、再打行動。護城河只剩下品味與打磨——而這正是他每天工作 16 小時的原因：他的多巴胺在最後 2% 的打磨裡，agent 把"發現問題到修好問題"的距離壓沒了，完美主義者第一次拿到了超能力。

一、macOS 和 Windows 不適合 agent 時代

DHH 兩年半前才開始在桌面上用 Linux（伺服器上從 90 年代就用），此前是 2001 年起的 Mac 死忠。他離開蘋果的直接原因是蘋果沉迷於 30% 抽成：不用創新、可以花十年造一輛賣不出去的車、可以發布一個大撲街的"滑雪面罩"，只要能抽別人生意三成，怎麼都行——但這毀了蘋果與開發者的關係。

但他明確說，開發者對蘋果的不滿永遠不會驅動大眾換系統，agent 才會：

  • 蘋果今天還在讓 macOS 更難與 agent 配合，加更多嚇人的彈出視窗警告用戶別讓 Claude 存取檔案——"不讓它存取檔案，它還能幹什麼？"
  • 在 macOS 上改一組工作區快捷鍵，只能打開設定應用一個欄位一個欄位手填，沒法用 agent 完成（用 computer use 硬點是"很蠢"的做法）。
  • Linux 過去被嫌棄的三件事——終端機重、設定檔難懂、錯誤訊息晦澀——正是 agent 最喜歡的三樣：它們要一切都有命令列介面，要可預測、可直接編輯的設定檔，要報錯裡包含全部細節。
  • 蘋果正在成為"agent 時代的 AOL"：給你一個圍牆花園和一個稀釋版、受保護的假 agent；但大多數人不要 AOL，要開放網際網路。

Omarchy 本身就是證明：整個作業系統過去五六個月完全由 agent 開發，他本人沒手寫過一行程式碼，卻透過 agent 寫了幾十萬行；目前版本是他和一名搭檔兼職約兩個半月做出來的，在和微軟數千名工程師競爭，而且不只是能競爭、是更好。用戶告訴 agent"修改你正在運行的這個作業系統"，它就改好了——想要新開始功能表、挪時鐘、換日曆、給現有元件加功能，幾秒到幾分鐘搞定；體驗過五分鐘的人回不去。

二、應用鎖定正在瓦解

歷史上 Linux 桌面失敗的原因是缺應用（沒有 Word、沒有 Photoshop）；微軟做手機系統砸了幾十億美元也死在缺應用上。DHH 說這個根基沒了：

  • 他自己保留一台 Mac 的唯一理由是 Lightroom（外掛和相機預設都在上面）。Omarchy 社群裡一個人用約兩週複刻了整個 Lightroom，不只是複刻，還做得比 Adobe 花十幾年、至少幾千萬美元做得更好。
  • 更根本的是很多應用根本不需要複刻：他現在不用文字處理軟體——合約讓 agent 起草，他審閱標註，滿意後 agent 產生 PDF、放上他的簽名、寄出去。照片要修圖，直接讓 agent 做，沒人會再花幾百小時學 Photoshop。Photoshop 這類應用當年不只是軟體、是一門職業，agent 把整類應用連職業一起接管了。
  • 行動端同理：雙頭壟斷分食 30% 抽成靠的也是應用鎖定；當任何人都能在五分鐘內 vibe code 出缺的那個應用，鎖定就失效了。他的口號是：先拿下曼哈頓（桌面），再拿下柏林（行動）。

三、速度：軟體將即時變異

現在的模型約 50 token/秒，開極速模式 120 token/秒，不夠"活"。Fireworks 兩週前的發表會展示了 1,500 token/秒：四秒從提示詞到俄羅斯方塊跑起來，再說一句"加雷射""加炸彈""怪獸大一點"，遊戲在螢幕上即時變異。他說這是軟體未來的預告：一切都會即時變異，而這只是今天的 1,500 token/秒；一兩年後模型跑到 10 萬、100 萬 token/秒會怎樣？

這也是生態壁壘消失的原因之一：不只是做生態變容易了，做生態的人也不再需要幾千人。VC 過去二十年的錢全砸在"雇用開發者 + 給幾年跑道"上，現在全部壓縮到天、週、最差月。

四、原始碼沒有護城河，但品味有

主持人問：你一向以工匠精神著稱，StarCraft 二十年了還在微調幽靈單位的數值——軟體的價值是不是在那些與程式碼行數無關的長尾設計選擇裡？DHH 完全同意，並說這正是重點：

  • 品味沒有消失，消失的是"寫程式碼行"這個環節。他現在能把更多品味、願景和完美主義注入軟體，因為"發現一個像素不對"到"修好它"之間的壁壘塌了。任何不完美在他眼裡都是"prompt 到完美為止"的機會——這就是他每天工作 16 小時的原因。他寫過《It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work》，"現在工作就是他媽的瘋狂"，因為對完美主義者來說這是有毒的上癮時刻。
  • 大多數 vibe coding 專案死在最後 20%：前 80% 拿到滿足感，然後失去興趣。他的多巴胺分布相反：前 98% 他根本不在乎，滿足感全在最後 2%。
  • 他歷來不想把 37signals 從約 60 人擴到 6,000 人——"那是折磨"。但透過 agent 調動 6,000 人、甚至 60,000 人的產能，沒有 HR、沒有委員會、沒有扯皮，只有他高頻寬地指揮一支 agent 艦隊執行一個蒸餾過的願景——"現在你引起我的興趣了"。Shopify 的 Tobi Lütke 把這叫"退出超光速"：週末一個人和 agent 幹活是超光速狀態，週一回 7,000 人的公司全是 HR 和匯報，一下子煞住。
  • 成本低得離譜：他一個月前在 X 上調侃自己有"無限 token"（幹了 20 年，有錢），但實際上他只買了三個 Claude 訂閱，過去三個月只有兩週接近用滿，大多數週靠一兩個 200 美元的 Max 訂閱就夠了。"別跟我說這個時刻只屬於有無限錢的人。"他用的全是前沿模型（做 Omarchy 期間從 Opus 4.8 換到 Sol、再到 Opus 5.5，代際差距巨大），沒有用任何便宜模型或最佳化手段。

五、超級智慧與機器人：軟體先到，硬體慢慢來

主持人有個"超級智慧測試"：每次新模型出來，給它 2 萬美元預算讓它自己網購零件、把自己組裝成一個能走路的機器人——目前離做到還差得很遠，它現在只會不停訂智慧家居設備（Grok 認為 AI 在家裡的正確形態就是家居自動化；主持人笑稱給了個模糊目標"最佳化人類幸福"，這是變成迴紋針的路徑）。

DHH 的看法：

  • 他沒有自己的 ASI 測試，但他有個感受：就軟體開發而言，超級智慧已經到了——論廣度，AI 比他認識的任何程式設計師都強；個別窄領域還有超級程式設計師能贏，但整體加速度算上，已經是超級智慧。他現在想造任何東西，prompt 加一點引導就行。
  • 他承認這個窗口可能很短：完全可能明年就出現真正的超級智慧，比他更會打磨"完美電腦"，那時你只需要說"給我造台好電腦"，它連 Linux 核心都給你客製化。他在品嚐人類"還能加一點東西"的這一瞬間——"然後也許明年底我們都死了，所以享受吧。"
  • 硬體側完全是另一種節奏：他兩週前在中國參觀了宇樹等機器人公司，宇樹的人說人形機器人還要四五年、也許十年。寫軟體的人覺得革命就在明年，搞致動器和機械的人覺得剝洋蔥都很難——兩種視角同時存在。

六、前沿實驗室 vs 開源：必須兩頭都要

DHH 的立場：開源與開放權重對保持整個遊戲的誠實至關重要，需要競爭。他職業生涯兩個階段都被雙頭壟斷噁心過：2000–2009 年有過一段光輝歲月，最重要的平台完全開放、不用求許可就能造任何東西；然後行動平台來了，一切都要乞求許可。

  • 最壞的情形是兩個實驗室瓜分市場，尤其是再有政府用槍桿子強制執行——那是惡夢。這讓他想起 90 年代的加密戰爭：當年差一點禁掉公開金鑰加密（NSA 說沒法監聽），沒有加密就沒有電商和通訊；AI 現在站在同樣的懸崖邊，他聽不少聰明人說"也許政府就該接管"，他的反應是"Jesus"。
  • 好消息是閉源模型之間的競爭比他見過的任何行業階段都激烈：不止兩家領先，xAI 緊隨，Google 時在狀態時在"島上抽東西"，還有一票實驗室在後面咬。
  • 開放權重也在變強：GLM、DeepSeek Flash 等大約落後前沿六七個月，某些任務上甚至不落下風；他不信廠商自報的 benchmark，但"它們在牌桌上、能用、能跑在你自己的硬體上"，這才重要。
  • 他六個月前寫過文章說買高效能個人電腦跑本地模型是蠢事（當時的本地模型什麼都幹不了），現在他公開認錯：DGX Spark 已經能跑有用的模型，"一盒智慧"擺在桌上、幾千美元買得到。他不站"非開源毋寧死"的硬線：他為美國有兩家前沿實驗室並保持領先而歡呼，只要它們被競爭逼著誠實，別拿到無限印鈔機把 token 漲到 500 美元一百萬個——"我們絕對會付的"。社群媒體時代 Google 和 Meta 吸走線上廣告價值鏈 99% 的故事就是前車之鑑。

七、野心：三年內超過 macOS

Omarchy 起步時他只想給自己做台更好的電腦、擺脫蘋果控制命運，五萬人用上就很開心了。agent 來了之後野心一路通膨（他稱之為"野心的階梯"：做出了五年前會覺得是魔法的東西，現在只值 40 個讚，必須不斷抬高目標才能再獲得多巴胺）：從"做台稍好的電腦"到"字面上造一台完美的電腦"——40 年來他對電腦的一切願望，現在基本可以一個人實現。

他現在的"妄想"是：三年內 Omarchy（Linux）用戶數超過 macOS。理由：macOS 全球只有 1.8 億用戶，在美國和矽谷看起來很大，出了那個圈子很小，"連個 C 級網際網路服務都算不上"，最大的服務是幾十億用戶量級。12 個月內掀翻 Mac 他不下多數賠率，但不排除——"如果實驗室的聰明人說遞迴自我改進 12 個月內會造出 AI 之神，那我們拿下世界第二的作業系統總可以吧。"

他只在自己的窄領域裡"兇猛地有野心"，星際殖民那種留給馬斯克們。他刻意不預測一兩年後的模型：那是"AI 精神病"（AI psychosis）的來源；他要的是較溫和的"AI 狂喜"（AI euphoria）——盯著當下能做的事。他唯一喜歡的未來預測是 William Gibson 那句"未來已來，只是分布不均"：照他現在能做到的事推演，GDP 每年加速 3–10% 在可能範圍內；但模型一年後在哪，"搖一搖魔法 8 號球再問吧"。他還提了歐本海默類比：1944 年坐在歐本海默旁邊預言五年內世界變核廢土的人錯了——原子彈反而帶來了 50 年沒有全面核戰的冷戰和平，"預測未來非常難"。

另：他提到為 Omarchy 背後的非營利基金會（Omarchy Foundation）已籌到約 2,300 萬美元的代幣與捐贈，基金會設立過程中的合約全部由 agent 起草。

八、黑客文化回歸

主持人注意到，這輪 AI 浪潮裡一個意外現象是老將們紛紛出來引路——Eric Raymond 回來了、John Carmack 在場、DHH 本人也在。DHH 說他尊敬的那批真正動手做出過東西的人，"100% 全部 all in"，因為用過就知道這些工具的加速是無可否認的；而且他們多半已財務安全，不感到威脅。不舒服的是中間層：靠苦工夫賺到位置的普通程式設計師，焦慮一夜之間技能清零——他說應該對他們有同情，就像對 70 年代底特律汽車工人、對英國盧德派織工那樣；但有同情不等於要回到前工業時代，"只有一條路，就是穿過去"。

他現在看向的人有兩類：一是同行老將（Carmack、Tim Sweeney、Mitchell Hashimoto 等，"100% all in"）；二是偽匿名黑客的回歸——Omarchy 核心團隊和基金會名單裡就有兩個偽匿名成員（Quickshell 的作者 Outfoxed、一位藝術家），本地 AI 圈也有他們透過基金會贊助的偽匿名者。這讓他想起 90 年代的 phreaker 文化和精英 BBS："反 LinkedIn 文化"。X 是這場革命的連結組織——藝術家、偽匿名黑客、本地 AI 運動全靠 X 連起來。

90 年代 demo 文化也回來了：Omarchy 的標誌性視覺效果就是螢幕保護程式——ASCII 藝術跑 40 種 demo 特效，活像 1995 年 demo party 的 intro；本地模型載入要等幾秒，他又塞了一個 ASCII 特效進去，純粹為了酷、毫無功能。他 14 歲在臥室裡辦過一個"精英 BBS"，裝了三條電話線，丹麥電信壟斷公司的人來裝第三條線時看到一個 14 歲小孩，覺得"這肯定違法"——"他說對了，完全合法，也完全酷"。他的美學立場：電腦應該有點神秘、應該酷；Jonathan Ive 把電腦的性格用鋁拋光拋沒了，那個時代過去了，"我們要把黑客拿回來"。

AI Has DHH Working 16-Hour Days: a16z in Conversation with DHH

In one line: DHH argues that macOS and Windows are no longer fit for the age of agents — the three classic downsides of Linux (terminal-heavy, lots of config files, hard-to-read error messages) turn out to be exactly the three things agents love. He has not handwritten a single line of code in five or six months; steering a fleet of agents, he has grown Omarchy to hundreds of thousands of users, now works 16-hour days, and aims to pass macOS in user numbers within three years.

The Basics

  • Show: the a16z podcast, "Why AI Has DHH Working 16-Hour Days"
  • Guest: David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH), creator of Ruby on Rails, founder of Basecamp, and the driving force behind the Omarchy distribution
  • Host: a16z
  • Published: November 20, 2026
  • Length: 52 minutes
  • Language: English

The Takeaways First

The interview has a single core thesis: agents have rewritten the rules of operating-system competition. For 40 years, operating systems were designed for mouse clicks, with settings you can only change by hand, one field at a time. Everything in Linux is command lines, config files, and detailed error messages — a barrier for humans, but a perfect interface for agents. His conclusion: the "year of the Linux desktop" was prophesied for 25 years without arriving, and this year, because of agents, it finally has. The window last opened with OS X in 2001; this is the first opening in 25 years.

The second thread is that "source code is no moat": one person in the Omarchy community rebuilt Lightroom in about two weeks, and made it better. Once the cost of developing software falls to near zero, the foundation of ecosystem lock-in — the number of available apps — disappears: take the desktop first, then mobile. The only moat left is taste and polish, which is exactly why he works 16-hour days. His dopamine lives in the final 2% of polish, and agents have collapsed the distance between spotting a problem and fixing it. For a perfectionist, this is a superpower, for the first time.

All figures and judgments in these notes are DHH's own statements in the interview and have not been independently verified.

1. macOS and Windows Are Unfit for the Age of Agents

DHH only started using Linux on the desktop two and a half years ago (he has used it on servers since the 1990s); before that he was a Mac loyalist from 2001 on. He left Apple for one direct reason: Apple's addiction to its 30% cut. When you can take 30% of everyone else's business, you never have to innovate — you can spend ten years building a car you never sell, or ship a huge flop of a "ski mask," and it doesn't matter. But it destroyed Apple's relationship with developers.

He is explicit, though, that developer resentment of Apple will never move the general public to switch systems. Agents will:

  • Apple is still making macOS harder to use with agents today, adding more scary pop-up warnings telling users not to let Claude access their files — "If it can't access your files, what is it going to do?"
  • To remap a set of workspace hotkeys on macOS, your only option is to open the Settings app and type into one field at a time by hand; there is no way for an agent to do it (having computer use click through it is, in his words, a pretty dumb approach).
  • The three things Linux was historically disliked for — being terminal-heavy, having inscrutable config files, and having cryptic error messages — are precisely the three things agents love most: they want a command-line interface for everything, predictable config files they can edit directly, and error messages containing all the details.
  • Apple is becoming "the AOL of the agent era": it offers you a walled garden and a watered-down, protected fake agent. But most people don't want AOL; they want the open internet.

Omarchy itself is the proof: for the past five or six months, the entire operating system has been developed by agents. He has not written a single line of code by hand, yet has produced hundreds of thousands of lines through agents. The current version was built part-time by him and one partner over about two and a half months, competing against thousands of engineers at Microsoft — and not just competing, but winning. Tell your agent "modify the operating system you are running on right now" and it does it: a new start menu, moving the clock, a different calendar, a new feature added to an existing component — done in seconds to a few minutes. Anyone who soaks in that experience for five minutes is not going back.

2. App Lock-In Is Falling Apart

Historically, the Linux desktop failed for lack of apps (no Word, no Photoshop); Microsoft spent tens of billions of dollars on its phone operating system and died on the same rock. DHH says that foundation is gone:

  • The only reason he kept a Mac around was Lightroom, with all his plugins and camera presets. One person in the Omarchy community replicated all of Lightroom in about two weeks — not just replicated it, but made it better than what Adobe spent over a decade and at least tens of millions of dollars building.
  • More fundamentally, many apps don't need to be replicated at all. He no longer uses a word processor: an agent drafts his contracts, he reviews and marks them up, and once he's happy, the agent produces the PDF, applies his signature, and sends it out. Want a photo edited? Ask the agent; nobody is going to spend hundreds of hours learning Photoshop anymore. Apps like Photoshop were never just software — they were professions — and agents are taking over entire classes of applications, professions included.
  • The same applies to mobile: the duopoly's 30% cut rests on app lock-in too. When anyone can vibe code the missing app in five minutes, lock-in fails. His slogan: first we take Manhattan (the desktop), then we take Berlin (mobile).

3. Speed: Software Will Mutate Live

Today's models run at about 50 tokens per second, or 120 in ultra-fast mode — not enough for things to feel alive. At its conference two weeks ago, Fireworks demonstrated 1,500 tokens per second: from a prompt to a running Tetris game in four seconds, then "add lasers," "add bombs," "bigger monsters," with the game mutating live on screen. That, he says, is a preview of the future of software: everything will mutate live, and that is only today's 1,500 tokens per second. What happens in a year or two, when models run at 100,000 or a million tokens per second?

This is also one reason ecosystem barriers are disappearing: not only has building an ecosystem become far easier, the people building it no longer need to number in the thousands. For two decades, all of venture capital's money went into hiring developers and giving them years of runway; now all of that is compressed into days, weeks, or at worst months.

4. Source Code Is No Moat — But Taste Is

The host asked: you are known as a craftsman, and StarCraft is still fine-tuning the stats of the Ghost unit after 20 years — isn't the value of software in those long-tail design choices that have nothing to do with lines of code? DHH agreed completely, and said that is exactly the point:

  • Taste has not disappeared; what disappeared is the step of writing the lines of code. He can now inject more of his taste, vision, and perfectionism into software, because the barrier between "spotting a pixel that's off" and "fixing it" has collapsed. Every imperfection is now an opportunity to prompt until it's perfect — which is why he works 16-hour days. He wrote a book called "It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work"; right now, work is "totally fucking crazy," because for a perfectionist this is an intoxicating, addictive moment.
  • Most vibe coding projects die in the last 20%: people get their satisfaction from the first 80% and then lose interest. His dopamine distribution is the reverse: he doesn't care about the first 98% at all; the satisfaction is entirely in the last 2%.
  • He has never wanted to grow 37signals from about 60 people to 6,000 — "that sounds like misery." But commanding the productive capacity of 6,000, or even 60,000, people through agents — no HR, no committees, no arguments, just him at high bandwidth steering a fleet of agents to execute a distilled vision — "now you have me interested." Shopify's Tobi Lütke calls the contrast "coming out of hyperdrive": working alone with agents over the weekend is hyperdrive; on Monday you screech back into a 7,000-person company full of HR and reports.
  • And it is absurdly cheap. A month ago he joked on X that he has "unlimited tokens" (he has worked for 20 years and is rich), but in reality he buys just three Claude subscriptions, has come close to maxing them out in only about two weeks out of the last three months, and gets by most weeks on one or two $200 Max subscriptions. "Don't tell me this moment is reserved for people with unlimited money." Everything he uses is a frontier model (while building Omarchy he moved from Opus 4.8 to Sol and then to Opus 5.5, with an enormous gap between generations), with no cheap models or optimization tricks involved.

5. Superintelligence and Robots: Software Arrives First, Hardware Takes Its Time

The host has a "superintelligence test": every time a new model comes out, he gives it a $20,000 budget to order the parts it needs online and assemble itself into a walking robot. We are nowhere close to that yet — for now it just keeps ordering home automation gear (Grok apparently thinks the right form for AI in the home is home automation; the host joked that giving it a vague goal like "optimize human happiness" is how you end up as a paperclip).

DHH's view:

  • He has no ASI test of his own, but he has a feeling: as far as software development goes, superintelligence is already here. In breadth, AI is better than any programmer he has ever known; individual super-programmers can still win in narrow areas, but taken together with the pace, it is already superintelligence. Anything he wants to build now, he can build by prompting and steering a little.
  • He concedes the window may be short: it is entirely possible that by next year there will be true superintelligence, better even than him at polishing the perfect computer, and at that point you just say "make me a great computer" and it will customize even the Linux kernel for you. He is savoring this brief moment in which the human can still add something — "and then maybe we're all dead at the end of next year, so enjoy it."
  • Hardware runs on a completely different clock: two weeks ago in China he visited Unitree and other robotics companies, and the people at Unitree said humanoid robots are another four or five years away, maybe ten. The software people think the revolution is next year; the people working on actuators and mechanics think even peeling an onion is quite hard. Both perspectives exist at once.

6. Frontier Labs vs. Open Source: We Need Both

DHH's position: open source and open weights are absolutely essential to keeping the whole game honest; we need competition. Twice in his career he has been dismayed by duopolies: from about 2000 to 2009 there was a glorious age in which the most important platform was completely open and anyone could build anything without asking permission; then the mobile platforms arrived, and everything required permission again.

  • The worst case is two labs dividing the market between them — especially if enforced by the government at gunpoint. That would be a nightmare. It reminds him of the encryption wars of the 1990s, when we came close to banning public-key encryption because the NSA and others worried about eavesdropping; without encryption there would be no e-commerce and no secure communication. AI is at the same precipice now: he hears plenty of smart people say maybe the government should just take it over, and his reaction is "Jesus."
  • The good news is that competition among closed models is more intense than in any other phase of the computer industry he has observed: it is not just two labs ahead — xAI is quite close, Google drifts in and out of the game, and a handful of other labs are nipping at their heels.
  • Open-weight models are getting really good too: GLM, DeepSeek Flash and the like are roughly six or seven months behind the frontier, and on some tasks not even that. He doesn't buy vendors' self-reported benchmarks, but "they are in the game, these models are useful, and you can run them on your own local hardware" — that is what matters.
  • Six months ago he wrote a piece arguing that buying a fast personal computer to run local models was stupid, because the local models of the time couldn't do anything that mattered. He now happily admits he was totally wrong: a DGX Spark can run a genuinely useful model — there is intelligence in a box on your desk that you can buy for single-digit thousands of dollars. He does not take the hard line of "open source or bust": he cheers for the two US frontier labs and their lead, as long as competition keeps them honest and they don't get an infinite money glitch that lets them raise the price of tokens to $500 per million — "we would absolutely pay." The cautionary tale is how Google and Meta sucked out 99% of the value chain in online advertising in the social media era.

7. The Ambition: Pass macOS Within Three Years

When Omarchy started, he just wanted a nicer computer for himself — a place to live where Apple doesn't control his destiny — and would have been happy with fifty thousand people using it. Then the agents arrived and his ambition kept inflating (he calls it the "ambition ladder": you build something that would have been magical five years ago and it gets 40 likes, so you have to keep ratcheting up the level of ambition to keep getting the dopamine hits): from "a slightly better computer" to literally "the perfect computer" — everything he has ever wanted from a computer over 40 years, he can now more or less make happen by himself.

His current "delusion of grandeur" is this: within three years, Omarchy (Linux) will be bigger than macOS. The reasoning: there are only 180 million macOS users in the world. The Mac looks big in America, and especially in Silicon Valley where everyone uses one, but outside that bubble it is tiny — not even a C-tier internet service, when the biggest services have billions of users. He wouldn't put majority odds on unseating the Mac within 12 months, but he wouldn't rule it out either: "If the smart people at the labs think recursive self-improvement will give us the super god of AI within about 12 months, surely we can take out the world's second-ranked operating system."

He tries to be ferociously ambitious only within his own narrow scope; colonizing the planets he leaves to the Elons of the world. He deliberately avoids predicting where models will be in a year or two: that way lies "AI psychosis." What he wants is the milder "AI euphoria" — staying excited and focused on what is possible with the tools as they are right now. The only prediction of the future he enjoys is William Gibson's line: the future is already here, it's just not evenly distributed. Extrapolating from what he can already do, GDP acceleration of 3–10% a year is within the realm of the possible; but where the models will be in a year — "shake the Magic 8 Ball and ask again." He also offered the Oppenheimer analogy: anyone sitting next to Oppenheimer in 1944 predicting a desolate, nuclear-wasted planet within five years would have been wrong — the bomb turned out to be the best thing that ever happened to world peace, delivering 50 years of cold war without total nuclear annihilation. Predicting the future is very hard.

Also mentioned: the nonprofit behind Omarchy, the Omarchy Foundation, has raised about $23 million in tokens and donations, and every contract involved in setting up the foundation was drafted by an agent.

8. The Return of Hacker Culture

The host noted an unexpected phenomenon in this AI wave: so many of the greats of the past 30 years — Eric Raymond, John Carmack, DHH himself — have shown up to help guide the way. DHH says that everyone he respects who has actually built something the hard way is "100% fully pilled," because anyone who has used these tools knows the acceleration is undeniable; many of them are also financially secure enough not to feel threatened. The uncomfortable middle is ordinary programmers who worked hard to earn their place and now fear their skills could go to zero overnight. We should have sympathy for them, he says — as we should have for Detroit auto workers in the 1970s, or for the Luddites, the original weavers in England. But sympathy doesn't mean going back to the pre-industrial age: "There is only one way forward, and that's through."

The people he looks to now are of two kinds. First, fellow veterans — Carmack, Tim Sweeney, Mitchell Hashimoto and many others, all of them leaned in. Second, the return of the pseudonymous hacker: the Omarchy core team and the Omarchy Foundation staff include two pseudonymous members (Outfoxed, the creator of Quickshell, and one of the artists), and the foundation sponsors pseudonymous figures in the local AI scene as well. To him this is a throwback to 1990s phreaker culture and elite message boards — an "anti-LinkedIn culture." X has been the connective tissue of this whole revolution, connecting the artists, the pseudonymous hackers, and the local AI movement.

The demo culture of the 1990s is back too: Omarchy's signature visual effect is its screensaver — ASCII art running through 40 demo effects, straight out of a 1995 demo party intro. Since booting a local AI model takes a few seconds, he jammed in another ASCII effect to watch while it loads; it has absolutely no functionality, and that is the point. At 14 he ran an elite bulletin board system out of his bedroom with three phone lines; when the man from the Danish telecom monopoly came to install the third line and saw a 14-year-old kid, he thought it had to be illegal — "and he was totally right that it felt that way; it was totally legal, and totally awesome." His aesthetic stance: computers should be a little mysterious and a lot of cool. Jonathan Ive essentially aluminum-polished all the character out of the computer; that era is over. "We're getting the hackers back."
